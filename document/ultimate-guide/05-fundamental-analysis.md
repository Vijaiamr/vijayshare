# 1. COMPANY OVERVIEW

## 1.1 Company History — How to Research and Analyze

Understanding a company's history is the foundation of fundamental analysis. History reveals management philosophy, resilience through cycles, and strategic direction.

**Sources for Company History:**

| Source | What You Find | How to Access |
|--------|---------------|---------------|
| Annual Reports | Detailed business history, milestones | Company website / BSE/NSE filings |
| IPO Prospectus (DRHP/RHP) | Founding story, pre-IPO financials | SEBI / Stock Exchange websites |
| Company Website | About Us, Timeline | Direct |
| News Archives | Major events, controversies | Google News, Economic Times |
| Industry Reports | Company's position in industry evolution | CRISIL, ICRA, CARE |
| Management Interviews | Vision, philosophy, strategy | YouTube, Conferences, Earnings Calls |
| Court Records | Legal battles, regulatory actions | Indian Kanoon, Court websites |

**Key Historical Metrics to Track:**

```
Founding Year:         When was the company established?
IPO Year:             When did it go public?
Key Milestones:       Major product launches, acquisitions, expansions
Survived Crises:      Did it survive 2000, 2008, 2020?
Leadership Changes:   How many CEO changes? Stability?
Strategic Pivots:     Major business model changes
Regulatory Actions:   Any fines, bans, restrictions?
```

**Analysis Framework for Company History:**

1. **Longevity Check**: Companies surviving 20+ years have weathered multiple cycles
2. **Crisis Management**: How did the company perform during recessions?
3. **Growth Trajectory**: Was growth organic or acquisition-driven?
4. **Management Continuity**: Frequent CEO changes = red flag
5. **Strategic Consistency**: Does the company stick to its core or chase trends?

## 1.2 Business Model Analysis

A business model defines how a company creates, delivers, and captures value. Understanding the business model is critical because it determines margins, scalability, capital requirements, and competitive dynamics.

**Types of Business Models:**

| Business Model | Description | Examples | Key Characteristics |
|----------------|-------------|----------|---------------------|
| Manufacturing | Produces physical goods | Tata Steel, Maruti Suzuki | High CapEx, cyclical, inventory risk |
| Services | Provides intangible offerings | TCS, Infosys | Low CapEx, people-dependent, scalable |
| Platform/Ecosystem | Connects buyers and sellers | Zomato, Naukri | Network effects, winner-take-most |
| Subscription | Recurring revenue model | Zee5, Netflix | Predictable revenue, high retention |
| Franchise | License brand and model | McDonald's India | Asset-light, rapid scaling |
| Marketplace | Aggregator model | Amazon India, Flipkart | Scale advantages, logistics moat |
| Distribution | Sells others' products | Titan (watches), Retail chains | Margin-dependent, volume-driven |
| Licensing | IP monetization | Pharma companies, IT patents | High margin, patent cliff risk |
| Leasing/Asset-light | Own and rent assets | IRB Infrastructure | Predictable cash flows |
| Hybrid | Combination of above | Reliance (O2C + Retail + Telecom) | Diversified, complex valuation |

**Business Model Quality Assessment:**

```
Recurring Revenue:     % of revenue from repeat customers
Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC):  How much to acquire one customer?
Lifetime Value (LTV):  Total revenue from one customer over relationship
LTV:CAC Ratio:         > 3x is healthy
Gross Margin:          > 50% = strong moat potential
Capital Intensity:     CapEx/Revenue ratio — lower is better
Scalability:           Can revenue double without doubling costs?
Cyclicality:           Revenue variance over economic cycles
```

## 1.4 Revenue Sources — Concentration and Quality

**Revenue Quality Metrics:**

| Metric | Description | Ideal |
|--------|-------------|-------|
| Recurring Revenue % | Revenue from subscriptions, maintenance, AMC | > 40% |
| Top Customer Concentration | Revenue from largest customer | < 20% |
| Top 5 Customer Concentration | Revenue from top 5 customers | < 40% |
| Geographic Concentration | Revenue from single geography | < 60% |
| One-time Revenue | Non-recurring items (asset sales, tax credits) | < 5% |
| Revenue Diversification | Number of independent revenue streams | > 3 |

**Recurring vs One-Time Revenue:**

```
Recurring Revenue (High Quality):
├── Subscription fees (monthly/annual)
├── Maintenance contracts (AMC)
├── Service contracts
├── Licensing fees (renewable)
├── Retainer fees
└── Platform fees (transaction-based)

One-Time Revenue (Low Quality):
├── Asset sales
├── Government subsidies (non-recurring)
├── Tax reversals
├── Insurance claims received
├── Litigation settlements
└── Foreign exchange gains
```

**Revenue Recognition Red Flags:**
- Revenue growing but receivables growing faster
- Bill-and-hold arrangements
- Channel stuffing near quarter-end
- Revenue from related parties at above-market rates
- Aggressive percentage-of-completion accounting

## 1.5 Market Capitalization

**Market Capitalization = Current Share Price × Total Outstanding Shares**

**Classification and Characteristics:**

| Category | Market Cap Range (India) | Characteristics |
|----------|--------------------------|-----------------|
| Large Cap | > ₹20,000 Cr | Stable, lower risk, lower growth, covered by analysts |
| Mid Cap | ₹5,000–20,000 Cr | Moderate risk, moderate growth, some analyst coverage |
| Small Cap | ₹500–5,000 Cr | Higher risk, higher growth potential, limited coverage |
| Micro Cap | < ₹500 Cr | Highest risk, speculative, minimal coverage |

**Market Cap Variations:**

```
Market Cap (Equity Value)    = Share Price × Equity Shares
Enterprise Value (EV)        = Market Cap + Debt - Cash

Market Cap vs Enterprise Value:
  - Market Cap: What equity holders own
  - EV: Total cost to acquire the company (buy equity + pay debt - take cash)

EV is more relevant for:
  - Comparing companies with different capital structures
  - Acquisition analysis
  - Cross-border comparisons
```

**Float-Adjusted Market Cap:**

```
Free Float Market Cap = Market Cap × Free Float %
Free Float = Shares available for public trading
  (excludes promoter, government, strategic holdings)

Why it matters:
- Index weightage is based on free-float market cap
- Lower float = higher volatility
- Higher float = better liquidity
```

# 2. BUSINESS ANALYSIS

## 2.1 Business Segments — Segment-Wise Revenue and Profit Analysis

**Why Segment Analysis Matters:**

A company may look profitable overall, but one segment could be masking losses in another. Segment analysis reveals the true economic engine.

**Segment Reporting Requirements (Ind AS 108 / IFRS 8):**

Companies must report:
- Segment Revenue (external and inter-segment)
- Segment Profit/Loss
- Segment Assets
- Segment Liabilities
- Capital Expenditure by segment
- Depreciation by segment

**Segment Analysis Framework:**

```
For each business segment, analyze:
├── Revenue Contribution (% of total)
├── Revenue Growth (YoY, QoQ)
├── Segment Profit Margin
├── Segment Margin Trend (improving/deteriorating)
├── Capital Employed in Segment
├── Return on Segment Capital
├── Competitive Position in Segment
├── Regulatory Environment for Segment
├── Capital Intensity (CapEx required)
├── Working Capital Needs
└── Growth Prospects
```

**Example Segment Analysis Table:**

| Segment | Revenue (₹Cr) | Revenue % | Revenue Growth | Segment Profit | Segment Margin | Capital Employed |
|---------|---------------|-----------|----------------|----------------|----------------|------------------|
| Segment A | 15,000 | 60% | 12% | 3,000 | 20% | 20,000 |
| Segment B | 7,500 | 30% | 22% | 1,125 | 15% | 10,000 |
| Segment C | 2,500 | 10% | 5% | 250 | 10% | 5,000 |
| **Total** | **25,000** | **100%** | **14%** | **4,375** | **17.5%** | **35,000** |

**Key Insights:**
- Segment A is the cash cow (60% revenue, 20% margin)
- Segment B is the growth engine (22% growth, improving margins)
- Segment C is lagging and may need strategic review
- Total return on capital employed: 4,375/35,000 = 12.5%

## 2.2 Competitive Advantage / Economic Moat

An economic moat is a structural competitive advantage that protects a company's profits from competition. The wider the moat, the more sustainable the returns.

### Cost Advantage Moat

```
Source: Lower cost of production than competitors

Examples:
├── Walmart (supply chain efficiency)
├── Tata Steel (integrated mining operations)
├── Reliance Jio (scale-driven low cost)
└── TCS (large-scale delivery model)

How to Identify:
├── Compare gross margins with industry average
├── Analyze cost structure item by item
├── Check if cost advantage is structural or temporary
├── Verify sustainability against new entrants
└── Look at unit economics

Sustainability Factors:
├── Economies of scale (hard to replicate without similar scale)
├── Process innovation (Toyota Production System)
├── Location advantage (proximity to raw materials)
├── Proprietary technology (lower-cost production methods)
└── Learning curve (accumulated experience)
```

### Brand Moat

```
Source: Customer willingness to pay premium for brand

Examples:
├── Apple (premium pricing power)
├── Asian Paints (consumer trust)
├── Maruti Suzuki (dominance through brand)
└── Nestlé Maggi (brand loyalty in noodles)

How to Measure:
├── Price premium vs generic/private label competitors
├── Brand value (Interbrand, Brand Finance rankings)
├── Customer retention rates
├── Market share stability over time
└── Consumer surveys and NPS scores

Key Distinction:
├── Strong Brand = Can raise prices without losing customers
├── Weak Brand = Competing on price alone
└── Brand moat is only valuable if it translates to pricing power
```

### Network Effect Moat

```
Source: Value increases as more users join

Examples:
├── Visa/Mastercard (payment network)
├── Google Search (search quality improves with data)
├── Facebook/Instagram (social graph)
├── Indian Railways (infrastructure monopoly)
└── CDSL/NSDL (depository network)

Types:
├── Direct Network Effect: Each new user adds value to existing users
│   (e.g., WhatsApp — more contacts = more value)
├── Indirect Network Effect: More users attract more complementary products
│   (e.g., iOS App Store — more users attract more developers)
├── Data Network Effect: More data improves product quality
│   (e.g., Google Search — more searches = better results)
└── Platform Network Effect: Two-sided marketplaces
    (e.g., Amazon — more buyers attract more sellers)

How to Identify:
├── High switching costs for users
├── Chicken-and-egg problem for competitors
├── Value grows exponentially with user base
├── Winner-take-most dynamics in market
└── Network size is a barrier to entry
```

### Switching Cost Moat

```
Source: Cost (time, money, effort) of switching to competitor

Examples:
├── Banks (changing all auto-debits, checks, relationships)
├── Enterprise Software (SAP, Oracle — migration costs)
├── CRM Systems (Salesforce — data, workflows, integrations)
├── Utilities (electricity, gas — infrastructure dependency)
└── Insurance (portability issues, policy terms)

Types of Switching Costs:
├── Financial: Contractual penalties, setup costs
├── Technical: Data migration, system integration
├── Learning: Time to learn new system, training costs
├── Psychological: Comfort, habit, trust
└── Data: Losing history, preferences, customizations

Assessment Criteria:
├── How many months/years of effort to switch?
├── What is the direct cost of switching?
├── Is data portable between providers?
├── Are there contractual lock-ins?
└── What is the disruption to business/personal life?
```

### Intangible Assets Moat

```
Source: Patents, licenses, regulatory approvals, trade secrets

Examples:
├── Pharmaceutical Patents (Glenmark, Dr. Reddy's)
├── Spectrum (Jio, Airtel)
├── Mining Licenses (NMDC, Coal India)
├── Banking License (rare to obtain)
├── Airport Concessions (GMR, Adani)
└── Formula/Know-How (Coca-Cola recipe)

Assessment Factors:
├── Duration of protection (patent expiry dates)
├── Number of patents (broad protection)
├── Regulatory barriers (how hard to obtain license)
├── Trade secret protection (is it replicable?)
└── Geographic coverage of protection
```

**Moat Assessment Scorecard:**

| Moat Type | Strength (1-5) | Durability (1-5) | Breadth (1-5) | Total Score |
|-----------|----------------|-------------------|----------------|-------------|
| Cost Advantage | 4 | 3 | 4 | 11 |
| Brand | 5 | 4 | 3 | 12 |
| Network Effect | 3 | 5 | 5 | 13 |
| Switching Cost | 4 | 4 | 3 | 11 |
| Intangible Assets | 3 | 3 | 2 | 8 |
| **Total** | | | | **55/75** |

## 2.3 Market Share Analysis

**How to Calculate Market Share:**

```
Volume Market Share = Company Units Sold / Total Industry Units Sold × 100
Value Market Share = Company Revenue / Total Industry Revenue × 100
Profit Market Share = Company Profit / Total Industry Profit × 100

Example:
Company Revenue: ₹5,000 Cr
Industry Revenue: ₹50,000 Cr
Value Market Share = 5,000 / 50,000 × 100 = 10%
```

**Market Share Analysis Framework:**

| Metric | Current | 3Y Ago | 5Y Ago | Trend |
|--------|---------|--------|--------|-------|
| Volume Share | 12% | 10% | 8% | Growing |
| Value Share | 15% | 13% | 11% | Growing |
| Geographic Share (North) | 18% | 16% | 14% | Growing |
| Geographic Share (South) | 8% | 8% | 7% | Stable |
| Online Channel Share | 22% | 15% | 5% | Rapid growth |

**What Market Share Trends Indicate:**

```
Increasing Market Share:
├── Strong competitive position
├── Superior execution
├── Better product/service
├── Effective distribution
├── Pricing advantage
└── Gaining from competitors' weakness

Declining Market Share:
├── Competitive pressure
├── Product/service obsolescence
├── Distribution weakness
├── Pricing pressure
├── Changing consumer preferences
└── Management distraction

Stable Market Share (in growing market):
├── Maintaining competitive position
├── Growing in line with industry
├── No significant gains or losses
└── Stable competitive dynamics
```

## 2.4 Pricing Power

**What Pricing Power Indicates:**

Pricing power is the ability to raise prices without losing customers. It is one of the strongest indicators of a moat.

**Measuring Pricing Power:**

```
Method 1: Price Elasticity of Demand
  Price Elasticity = % Change in Quantity Demanded / % Change in Price
  If |Elasticity| < 1: Inelastic demand (pricing power exists)
  If |Elasticity| > 1: Elastic demand (no pricing power)

Method 2: Price Increase vs Volume Loss Analysis
  If company raised prices by 10% and volume dropped by 2%:
  Net Revenue Impact = +10% - 2% = +8% (positive)
  This indicates pricing power

Method 3: Margin Expansion During Inflation
  If input costs rise 8% and company raises prices by 10%:
  Gross margin expands → Company has pricing power

Method 4: Price Comparison vs Competitors
  If company consistently prices 20% above competitors and maintains
  market share → Strong pricing power
```

**Pricing Power Spectrum:**

| Level | Description | Example | Indication |
|-------|-------------|---------|------------|
| Very Strong | Can raise prices anytime without resistance | Apple | Wide moat |
| Strong | Can raise prices with minimal volume impact | Asian Paints | Narrow-Wide moat |
| Moderate | Can raise prices selectively | HUL | Narrow moat |
| Weak | Price increases cause significant volume loss | Generic pharma | No moat |
| None | Price taker, no control | Commodity companies | No moat |

## 2.5 Customer Base — Concentration and Type

**B2B vs B2C Analysis:**

| Factor | B2B (Business-to-Business) | B2C (Business-to-Consumer) |
|--------|---------------------------|---------------------------|
| Customer Base | Few, large customers | Many, small customers |
| Revenue Concentration | High (risk) | Low (safer) |
| Relationship | Long-term contracts | Transactional |
| Pricing | Negotiated, volume discounts | Fixed/market price |
| Switching Costs | Often high | Often low |
| Revenue Visibility | High (order book) | Lower (demand-driven) |
| Cyclicality | Tied to customer's business | Tied to consumer spending |
| Brand Importance | Moderate | Very High |

**Customer Concentration Risk Assessment:**

```
Risk Level:
├── Top 1 customer > 25% revenue: HIGH RISK
├── Top 1 customer 10-25% revenue: MODERATE RISK
├── Top 1 customer < 10% revenue: LOW RISK
├── Top 5 customers > 50% revenue: HIGH RISK
├── Top 5 customers 30-50% revenue: MODERATE RISK
└── Top 5 customers < 30% revenue: LOW RISK

Mitigation Factors:
├── Long-term contracts (5+ years)
├── Essential product/service (can't be easily replaced)
├── Diversification across industries
├── Strong relationship with multiple decision-makers
└── High switching costs for customer
```

## 2.6 Supplier Dependency

**Supplier Concentration Analysis:**

```
High Supplier Risk Indicators:
├── Single source for critical input
├── Supplier has pricing power
├── No alternative suppliers available
├── Input is unique/specialized
├── Supplier is also a competitor
└── Long lead times for switching

Low Supplier Risk Indicators:
├── Multiple qualified suppliers
├── Commodity/standardized inputs
├── Company is a major buyer (leverage)
├── Backward integration (self-manufacture)
├── Easy to qualify new suppliers
└── Low switching costs
```

**Backward Integration as a Moat:**

```
Example: Reliance Industries
├── Owns oil fields (upstream)
├── Owns refineries (midstream)
├── Owns petrochemical plants (downstream)
└── Result: End-to-end control, cost advantage

Example: Tata Steel
├── Owns iron ore mines
├── Owns coal mines
├── Owns limestone quarries
└── Result: Raw material cost advantage
```

```
├── Net Exposure: Commodity currency risk
```

## 2.8 Future Growth Opportunities

**Organic vs Inorganic Growth:**

| Factor | Organic Growth | Inorganic Growth (M&A) |
|--------|---------------|------------------------|
| Source | Internal operations, R&D | Acquisitions, mergers |
| Speed | Slower | Faster |
| Risk | Lower (known territory) | Higher (integration risk) |
| Cost | Lower (internal investment) | Higher (premium paid) |
| Control | Full control | Partial control |
| Culture | Preserved | May be disrupted |
| Track Record | Easier to assess | Harder to predict |
| Capital Efficiency | Higher (no premium) | Lower (goodwill) |

**Growth Opportunity Assessment Framework:**

```
For each growth opportunity, evaluate:
├── Market Size (TAM - Total Addressable Market)
├── Company's Current Market Share
├── Growth Rate of Market
├── Competitive Landscape
├── Required Investment (CapEx + Working Capital)
├── Expected Returns (ROIC vs WACC)
├── Time to Profitability
├── Risk Factors
├── Management Capability
└── Strategic Fit with Core Business
```

**Sustainable Growth Rate Formula:**

```
Sustainable Growth Rate = ROE × (1 - Dividend Payout Ratio)

Example:
ROE = 20%
Dividend Payout = 30%
Sustainable Growth Rate = 20% × (1 - 0.30) = 14%

This means the company can grow at 14% indefinitely without
increasing leverage or issuing new equity, while maintaining
its current dividend policy.
```

# 3. FINANCIAL STATEMENTS — COMPLETE DEEP DIVE

## 3.1 Income Statement (Profit & Loss Statement)

The income statement shows a company's financial performance over a specific period (quarter or year).

**Complete Income Statement Structure:**

```
Revenue (Net Sales)
(-) Excise Duty / GST (if applicable)
= Net Revenue
(-) Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)
= Gross Profit
(-) Employee Benefits Expense
(-) Other Expenses (Selling, General & Administrative)
= EBITDA (Earnings Before Interest, Tax, Depreciation & Amortization)
(-) Depreciation & Amortization
= EBIT (Operating Profit / PBIT)
(-) Interest Expense
(-) Other Non-Operating Income/Expense
= EBT (Earnings Before Tax)
(-) Tax Expense
= Net Profit (PAT - Profit After Tax)
(-) Minority Interest
= Profit Attributable to Parent Shareholders
(/) Number of Outstanding Shares
= Earnings Per Share (EPS)
```

### Revenue

**Revenue Recognition (Ind AS 115 / IFRS 15):**

```
Five-Step Model:
1. Identify the contract with the customer
2. Identify performance obligations
3. Determine the transaction price
4. Allocate transaction price to performance obligations
5. Recognize revenue when performance obligation is satisfied

When is Revenue Recognized?
├── Point in time: When control transfers (goods delivered)
├── Over time: When service is provided progressively
├── Percentage of completion: For long-term contracts
└── Completed performance: When all obligations met
```

**Revenue Quality Assessment:**

| Factor | High Quality | Low Quality |
|--------|-------------|-------------|
| Recurring | Yes (subscriptions, AMC) | No (one-time sales) |
| Cash-backed | High cash conversion | High receivables |
| Organic | Grown without acquisitions | Driven by acquisitions |
| Diversified | Multiple products/customers | Concentrated |
| Sustainable | Growing with market | Dependent on subsidies |
| Transparent | Clear recognition | Aggressive recognition |

### Gross Profit and Gross Margin

```
Gross Profit = Revenue - COGS
Gross Margin = Gross Profit / Revenue × 100

COGS Includes:
├── Raw Materials
├── Direct Labor
├── Manufacturing Overhead
├── Freight/Transport (inbound)
└── Duties and Taxes on production

COGS Excludes:
├── Selling expenses
├── Administrative expenses
├── Depreciation (sometimes included)
├── Interest expense
└── Tax expense

Interpretation:
├── High Gross Margin (>50%): Strong pricing power, brand, or IP
├── Medium Gross Margin (25-50%): Competitive industry, some differentiation
├── Low Gross Margin (<25%): Commodity, price competition, thin margins
└── Improving Gross Margin: Better pricing, cost control, product mix
```

### Operating Profit (EBIT)

```
EBIT = Revenue - COGS - Operating Expenses (SG&A, R&D)
EBIT = Operating Profit = PBIT (Profit Before Interest and Tax)

What EBIT Excludes:
├── Interest expense (financing decision)
├── Tax expense (government policy)
├── Non-operating income (one-time gains)
└── Non-operating expenses (impairments, write-offs)

What EBIT Includes:
├── All operating revenue
├── All operating costs
├── Depreciation and amortization (operating charge)
└── Provisions related to operations

Why EBIT Matters:
├── Shows operational profitability independent of capital structure
├── Allows comparison across companies with different debt levels
├── Removes tax jurisdiction differences
└── Focuses on core business performance
```

### EBITDA

```
EBITDA = EBIT + Depreciation + Amortization
EBITDA = Revenue - COGS - Operating Expenses (excluding D&A)

Why EBITDA is Used:
├── Proxies operating cash flow
├── Removes non-cash charges (D&A)
├── Allows comparison of capital-intensive businesses
├── Common in valuation (EV/EBITDA)
├── Used in credit analysis and covenant compliance

Limitations of EBITDA:
├── Ignores actual cash requirements (CapEx IS needed)
├── Can be misleading for growing companies (high CapEx)
├── Does not account for working capital changes
├── Management can manipulate through classification
├── Should NEVER be used as a proxy for free cash flow
└── Warren Buffett: "Does management think the tooth fairy pays for CapEx?"

Better Alternative: EBITDA - CapEx = Cash EBITDA (closer to actual cash generation)
```

### Net Profit (PAT)

```
Net Profit (PAT) = EBT - Tax Expense
PAT = Profit After Tax = Bottom Line

Quality of Earnings Assessment:
├── Is net profit backed by operating cash flow?
├── Are there significant non-recurring items inflating profit?
├── Is the tax rate normal or artificially low (deferred tax)?
├── Are minority interests eating into parent company profit?
├── Are there significant extraordinary items?
└── How does net profit growth compare to revenue growth?
```

### EPS (Basic vs Diluted)

```
Basic EPS = (PAT - Preference Dividend) / Weighted Average Number of Equity Shares

Diluted EPS = (PAT - Preference Dividend) / (Weighted Average Shares + Dilutive Potential Shares)

Dilutive Potential Shares Include:
├── Stock Options (ESOP)
├── Convertible Debentures
├── Convertible Preference Shares
├── Warrants
└── Convertible Loans

Why Diluted EPS Matters:
├── Shows worst-case EPS if all instruments converted
├── Essential for companies with large ESOP pools
├── Important for companies with convertible debt
├── Always compare Basic and Diluted EPS
└── Large gap = significant dilution risk
```

### Operating Margin and Net Margin Analysis

```
Operating Margin = EBIT / Revenue × 100
Net Margin = PAT / Revenue × 100

Margin Analysis Over Time:
├── Improving margins: Operational efficiency, pricing power, scale benefits
├── Declining margins: Cost pressure, competition, adverse product mix
├── Stable margins: Mature business, controlled costs
└── Volatile margins: Commodity exposure, one-time items

Cross-Company Comparison:
├── Compare within same industry (peer group)
├── Adjust for accounting policies (e.g., depreciation methods)
├── Consider capital intensity differences
├── Account for geographic mix differences
└── Factor in business model differences (B2B vs B2C)
```

## 3.2 Balance Sheet

The balance sheet is a snapshot of a company's financial position at a specific point in time.

**Complete Balance Sheet Structure:**

```
ASSETS
├── Non-Current Assets
│   ├── Property, Plant & Equipment (PP&E)
│   ├── Intangible Assets (Goodwill, Patents, Software)
│   ├── Capital Work-in-Progress (CWIP)
│   ├── Investment Property
│   ├── Long-term Investments
│   ├── Deferred Tax Assets
│   ├── Long-term Loans & Advances
│   └── Other Non-Current Assets
├── Current Assets
│   ├── Inventories
│   ├── Trade Receivables
│   ├── Cash & Cash Equivalents
│   ├── Short-term Investments
│   ├── Short-term Loans & Advances
│   └── Other Current Assets
└── Total Assets

LIABILITIES
├── Shareholders' Equity
│   ├── Share Capital
│   ├── Reserves & Surplus (Retained Earnings)
│   ├── Money Received Against Share Warrants
│   └── Other Equity
├── Non-Current Liabilities
│   ├── Long-term Borrowings
│   ├── Deferred Tax Liabilities
│   ├── Long-term Provisions
│   ├── Other Long-term Liabilities
│   └── Non-current Trade Payables
├── Current Liabilities
│   ├── Short-term Borrowings
│   ├── Trade Payables
│   ├── Other Current Liabilities
│   ├── Short-term Provisions
│   └── Current Maturities of Long-term Debt
└── Total Liabilities

Accounting Equation:
Total Assets = Total Liabilities + Shareholders' Equity
```

### Current Assets — Deep Dive

```
Cash & Cash Equivalents:
├── Most liquid asset
├── Includes: Cash in bank, treasury bills (< 3 months), money market funds
├── Red Flag: Cash declining while debt increasing
├── Green Flag: Cash growing faster than debt
└── Important: Check if cash is restricted or pledged

Trade Receivables:
├── Amounts owed by customers
├── Red Flag: Growing faster than revenue (collection issues)
├── Key Metric: Receivable Days = Receivables / Revenue × 365
├── Healthy: Stable or declining receivable days
└── Concerning: Receivable days increasing significantly

Inventory:
├── Stock of raw materials, WIP, finished goods
├── Red Flag: Inventory growing faster than revenue (demand slowdown)
├── Key Metric: Inventory Days = Inventory / COGS × 365
├── Concerning: Obsolete inventory not written off
└── Critical: Check inventory valuation method (FIFO/LIFO/WAC)
```

### Fixed Assets — Deep Dive

```
Property, Plant & Equipment (PP&E):
├── Land (not depreciated)
├── Buildings (depreciated over 20-40 years)
├── Plant & Machinery (depreciated over 10-20 years)
├── Furniture & Fixtures (depreciated over 5-10 years)
├── Vehicles (depreciated over 5-8 years)
├── Computer Equipment (depreciated over 3-5 years)
└── Leasehold Improvements (over lease term)

Depreciation Policies:
├── Straight Line Method (SLM): Equal charge each year
│   Annual Depreciation = (Cost - Residual Value) / Useful Life
├── Written Down Value (WDV): Higher charge early, declining over time
│   Depreciation = Rate × Book Value
└── Companies must disclose depreciation policy in Notes to Accounts

Capital Work-in-Progress (CWIP):
├── Assets under construction not yet ready for use
├── Not depreciated until placed in service
├── Red Flag: Very large CWIP relative to PP&E (potential capital misallocation)
├── Concerning: CWIP remaining CWIP for unusually long periods
└── May include: Under-construction factories, offices, infrastructure
```

### Liabilities — Deep Dive

```
Current Liabilities:
├── Short-term Debt (repayable within 1 year)
├── Trade Payables (amounts owed to suppliers)
├── Current Portion of Long-term Debt (maturing within 1 year)
├── Accrued Expenses (expenses incurred but not yet paid)
├── Advances from Customers (cash received before delivery)
├── Statutory Dues (TDS, GST payable, PF payable)
└── Short-term Provisions (anticipated expenses)

Long-term Liabilities:
├── Long-term Debt (bank loans, debentures, bonds)
├── Deferred Tax Liabilities (timing difference in tax)
├── Long-term Provisions (gratuity, leave encashment)
└── Other Long-term Liabilities (lease liabilities under Ind AS 116)
```

### Shareholders' Equity

```
Components:
├── Share Capital: Face value × Number of shares issued
├── Securities Premium: Amount received above face value
├── Retained Earnings: Cumulative undistributed profits
├── Other Comprehensive Income: Unrealized gains (securities fair value, etc.)
├── General Reserve: Appropriated from profits
└── Capital Redemption Reserve: Created on buyback of shares

What to Analyze:
├── Is share capital increasing? (dilution from new shares)
├── Is retained earnings growing? (profits being retained)
├── Any treasury shares? (company bought back shares)
├── Are there significant reserves? (accumulated wealth)
└── Is equity growing faster than debt? (healthy capital structure)
```

### Book Value

```
Book Value (Equity) = Total Assets - Total Liabilities
Book Value Per Share = Book Value / Number of Outstanding Shares

Tangible Book Value = Book Value - Intangible Assets - Goodwill
Tangible Book Value Per Share = Tangible Book Value / Shares Outstanding

When Book Value Matters:
├── Asset-heavy businesses (banks, manufacturing, real estate)
├── Mature companies with stable earnings
├── Companies trading near or below book value
└── Financial sector analysis

When Book Value Is Less Relevant:
├── Asset-light businesses (IT, services, platform companies)
├── Companies with significant goodwill from acquisitions
├── Companies with undervalued/overvalued assets on balance sheet
└── Growth companies where value is in future earnings
```

## 3.3 Cash Flow Statement

The cash flow statement shows actual cash movements — it cannot be manipulated as easily as the income statement.

**Complete Cash Flow Statement Structure:**

```
CASH FLOWS FROM OPERATING ACTIVITIES
├── Net Profit Before Tax
├── Adjustments for Non-Cash Items
│   ├── Depreciation & Amortization
│   ├── Provisions
│   ├── Loss on Sale of Assets
│   ├── Goodwill Impairment
│   ├── Deferred Tax
│   └── Interest Expense (added back here, shown in financing)
├── Operating Profit Before Working Capital Changes
├── Working Capital Changes
│   ├── (Increase)/Decrease in Receivables
│   ├── (Increase)/Decrease in Inventory
│   ├── (Increase)/Decrease in Loans & Advances
│   ├── Increase/(Decrease) in Payables
│   └── Increase/(Decrease) in Other Current Liabilities
├── Cash Generated from Operations
├── Income Tax Paid
└── Net Cash from Operating Activities

CASH FLOWS FROM INVESTING ACTIVITIES
├── Purchase of Fixed Assets (CapEx)
├── Sale of Fixed Assets
├── Purchase of Investments
├── Sale/Maturity of Investments
├── Acquisition of Subsidiaries
├── Loans Given to Third Parties
└── Net Cash from Investing Activities

CASH FLOWS FROM FINANCING ACTIVITIES
├── Proceeds from Borrowings
├── Repayment of Borrowings
├── Proceeds from Issue of Shares
├── Buyback of Shares
├── Dividends Paid
├── Interest Paid
└── Net Cash from Financing Activities

NET INCREASE/(DECREASE) IN CASH
Opening Cash Balance
Closing Cash Balance
```

### Operating Cash Flow Analysis

```
Direct Method (less common):
├── Cash received from customers
├── Cash paid to suppliers
├── Cash paid to employees
├── Cash paid for other expenses
└── Cash paid for taxes

Indirect Method (more common):
├── Start with net profit
├── Add back non-cash charges (D&A, provisions)
├── Adjust for working capital changes
└── Arrive at cash generated from operations

Quality of Operating Cash Flow:
├── OCF should be positive and growing
├── OCF should be close to or above net profit
├── OCF should cover CapEx requirements
├── OCF should be stable (not volatile)
└── OCF should not depend on one-time items
```

### Free Cash Flow (FCF)

```
Free Cash Flow = Operating Cash Flow - Capital Expenditure
FCF = OCF - CapEx

Variations:
├── FCFF (Free Cash Flow to Firm) = EBIT(1-Tax) + D&A - ΔWC - CapEx
├── FCFE (Free Cash Flow to Equity) = FCFF - Interest(1-Tax) + Net Borrowing
└── Owner Earnings (Buffett) = Net Profit + D&A - Maintenance CapEx

Interpretation:
├── Positive FCF: Company generates more cash than it needs
├── Negative FCF: Company is investing heavily (could be good or bad)
├── Growing FCF: Business is becoming more cash-generative
├── Declining FCF: Business may be maturing or deteriorating
└── FCF > Net Profit: High quality earnings (cash-backed)
```

### CapEx Analysis

```
Maintenance CapEx vs Growth CapEx:
├── Maintenance CapEx: Spending needed to maintain current operations
│   (replacing worn-out equipment, maintaining facilities)
│   Approximation: Depreciation amount
│
├── Growth CapEx: Spending to expand operations
│   (new factories, new markets, new products)
│   Total CapEx - Maintenance CapEx = Growth CapEx
│
└── Why it matters:
    Company with ₹100 Cr depreciation and ₹150 Cr CapEx:
    ├── Maintenance CapEx: ~₹100 Cr
    ├── Growth CapEx: ~₹50 Cr
    └── True economic profit higher than accounting profit suggests

Capital Intensity:
CapEx Intensity = CapEx / Revenue
├── Low (< 5%): IT Services, FMCG, Consumer
├── Medium (5-15%): Auto, Pharma, Retail
├── High (15-30%): Telecom, Infrastructure, Power
└── Very High (> 30%): Oil & Gas, Mining, Steel
```

### Cash Conversion Ratio

```
Cash Conversion Ratio = Operating Cash Flow / Net Profit

Interpretation:
├── Ratio > 1: Company collecting more cash than accounting profit (excellent)
├── Ratio = 1: Cash flow matches profit (healthy)
├── Ratio < 1: Some profit is tied up in receivables/inventory
├── Ratio < 0.5: Serious cash flow concern despite reported profits
└── Negative: Company losing cash despite reported profits (RED FLAG)

Example:
Net Profit: ₹500 Cr
Operating Cash Flow: ₹600 Cr
Cash Conversion Ratio = 600/500 = 1.2 (Excellent — cash flow exceeds profit)

Net Profit: ₹500 Cr
Operating Cash Flow: ₹200 Cr
Cash Conversion Ratio = 200/500 = 0.4 (Concerning — profit not backed by cash)
```

## 4.1 Profitability Ratios

### Return on Equity (ROE)

```
ROE = Net Profit / Shareholders' Equity × 100
ROE = PAT / Average Equity × 100 (using average for better accuracy)

What ROE Measures:
├── Return generated on shareholders' money
├── Efficiency of equity capital deployment
├── Overall profitability from equity perspective
└── Key metric for value investors

Industry Benchmarks:
├── Banking: 12-18%
├── IT Services: 25-40%
├── FMCG: 20-40%
├── Pharma: 15-25%
├── Auto: 15-25%
├── Manufacturing: 12-20%
└── Capital Goods: 12-20%

Red Flags:
├── ROE < 10% consistently: Poor capital efficiency
├── ROE declining over time: Deteriorating business
├── ROE driven by high leverage: Risky, not sustainable
└── ROE significantly higher than peers: Investigate accounting or leverage
```

### DuPont Analysis (ROE Breakdown)

```
3-Step DuPont:
ROE = Net Profit Margin × Asset Turnover × Equity Multiplier
ROE = (PAT/Revenue) × (Revenue/Total Assets) × (Total Assets/Equity)

5-Step DuPont:
ROE = Tax Burden × Interest Burden × EBIT Margin × Asset Turnover × Equity Multiplier
ROE = (PAT/EBT) × (EBT/EBIT) × (EBIT/Revenue) × (Revenue/Assets) × (Assets/Equity)

Each Component:
├── Tax Burden = PAT/EBT: How much profit is retained after tax
├── Interest Burden = EBT/EBIT: Impact of interest expense
├── EBIT Margin = EBIT/Revenue: Operational profitability
├── Asset Turnover = Revenue/Assets: Asset efficiency
└── Equity Multiplier = Assets/Equity: Leverage (1 + D/E ratio)

DuPont Example:
├── Net Profit Margin: 15% (₹150/₹1,000)
├── Asset Turnover: 0.8x (₹1,000/₹1,250)
├── Equity Multiplier: 2.0x (₹1,250/₹625)
├── ROE = 15% × 0.8 × 2.0 = 24%
│
└── Analysis: Company has decent margins, moderate asset efficiency,
    and uses 2x leverage. The leverage boosts ROE from 12% (unlevered)
    to 24% (levered). This is sustainable if interest coverage is strong.
```

### Return on Capital Employed (ROCE)

```
ROCE = EBIT / Capital Employed × 100
Capital Employed = Total Assets - Current Liabilities
Capital Employed = Equity + Long-term Debt

Why ROCE > ROE:
├── ROCE measures return on ALL capital (debt + equity)
├── ROE measures return only on EQUITY capital
├── ROCE is not affected by leverage decisions
├── ROCE provides better comparison across companies with different D/E
└── ROCE is preferred by European and value investors

Interpretation:
├── ROCE > WACC: Company creating value (returns exceed cost of capital)
├── ROCE < WACC: Company destroying value (returns below cost of capital)
├── ROCE > 15% consistently: Strong value creation
├── ROCE declining: Business quality deteriorating
└── Compare ROCE across cycles, not just current year
```

### Return on Assets (ROA)

```
ROA = Net Profit / Total Assets × 100

What ROA Measures:
├── How efficiently company uses its assets to generate profit
├── Asset utilization efficiency
├── Relevant for asset-heavy industries
└── Lower is better for required asset base

Industry Considerations:
├── IT Companies: High ROA (low asset base)
├── Banks: Low ROA (high asset base — loans are assets)
├── Manufacturing: Moderate ROA
└── Real Estate: Variable ROA (depends on inventory valuation)

ROA Decomposition:
ROA = Net Profit Margin × Asset Turnover
ROA = (PAT/Revenue) × (Revenue/Assets)
```

### Margin Analysis

```
Gross Margin = Gross Profit / Revenue × 100
├── Indicates production efficiency and pricing power
├── High and stable = strong competitive position
└── Declining = input cost pressure or pricing competition

Operating Margin = EBIT / Revenue × 100
├── Shows operational efficiency
├── Includes all operating costs
├── Most comparable across companies
└── Excludes financing and tax decisions

EBITDA Margin = EBITDA / Revenue × 100
├── Proxies cash operating profitability
├── Common in M&A and valuation
├── Ignores CapEx requirements
└── Overused — should be compared with cash EBITDA

Net Profit Margin = PAT / Revenue × 100
├── Bottom-line profitability
├── Includes all expenses, interest, and tax
├── Most comprehensive margin
└── Can be distorted by one-time items

Comparison Framework:
├── Gross Margin > 50%: Strong moat (tech, pharma, luxury)
├── Operating Margin > 20%: Efficient operations
├── EBITDA Margin > 25%: Strong cash generation potential
├── Net Margin > 15%: Excellent bottom-line profitability
└── Improving margins over time: Positive trend
```

## 4.2 Valuation Ratios

### Price-to-Earnings (PE) Ratio

```
PE Ratio = Market Price per Share / Earnings Per Share (EPS)
PE Ratio = Market Cap / Net Profit

Types:
├── Trailing PE (TTM): Based on last 12 months' earnings
├── Forward PE: Based on next 12 months' expected earnings
├── Shiller PE (CAPE): Based on inflation-adjusted 10-year average earnings
└── Relative PE: Company PE / Sector PE

Interpretation:
├── PE < 10: Potentially undervalued (or low growth expectations)
├── PE 10-15: Fair value range (for mature companies)
├── PE 15-25: Growth expectations priced in
├── PE 25-40: High growth expectations
├── PE > 40: Very high growth expectations (or overvalued)
└── Negative PE: Company reporting losses (not meaningful)

PE in Context:
├── Compare PE with sector average
├── Compare PE with company's historical PE range
├── Compare PE with growth rate (use PEG)
├── High PE + High Growth = May be justified
├── High PE + Low Growth = Overvalued
├── Low PE + High Growth = Potentially undervalued
└── Low PE + Low Growth = Value trap possible
```

### Forward PE

```
Forward PE = Current Price / Expected EPS (next 12 months)

How to Calculate Expected EPS:
├── Analyst consensus estimates
├── Company guidance
├── Historical growth rate extrapolation
└── Industry growth rate application

Advantages over Trailing PE:
├── Forward-looking (prices are based on future expectations)
├── Captures growth trajectory
├── More relevant for growth companies
└── Adjusts for known future events (new capacity, contracts)

Disadvantages:
├── Estimates can be wrong
├── Companies may guide conservatively
├── Market conditions may change
├── Analyst estimates can be biased
└── Less reliable for cyclical companies

Example:
Current Price: ₹500
Current EPS (TTM): ₹25
Expected EPS (Next Year): ₹30
Trailing PE = 500/25 = 20x
Forward PE = 500/30 = 16.7x
PE implies 20% earnings growth expected
```

### PEG Ratio

```
PEG Ratio = PE Ratio / Earnings Growth Rate (%)

Interpretation:
├── PEG < 1: Potentially undervalued (growth not fully priced)
├── PEG = 1: Fairly valued (price matches growth)
├── PEG > 1.5: Potentially overvalued (growth premium too high)
├── PEG > 2: Significantly overvalued (growth expectations too optimistic)
└── PEG < 0.5: Deep value (if growth is sustainable)

Example:
Company A: PE = 30, Growth = 25%
PEG = 30/25 = 1.2 (slightly overvalued)

Company B: PE = 15, Growth = 20%
PEG = 15/20 = 0.75 (potentially undervalued)

Company B is cheaper on PEG basis despite lower PE.

Limitations:
├── Growth rate choice significantly impacts PEG
├── Not suitable for negative earnings or negative growth
├── Assumes growth rate is linear and sustainable
├── Does not account for risk (volatility of earnings)
├── Works best for growth companies, less for value
└── One-year growth can be misleading; use 3-5 year CAGR
```

### Price-to-Book (P/B) Ratio

```
P/B Ratio = Market Price per Share / Book Value per Share
P/B Ratio = Market Cap / Shareholders' Equity

When P/B is Relevant:
├── Banks and Financial Institutions (assets are mark-to-market)
├── Real Estate companies (land/buildings on balance sheet)
├── Insurance companies
├── Asset-heavy manufacturing
├── Companies with tangible assets
└── Mature, stable-earnings companies

When P/B is NOT Relevant:
├── IT/Services companies (minimal physical assets)
├── Companies with large goodwill
├── Companies with negative equity
├── Intensive R&D companies
└── Platform/ecosystem businesses

Interpretation:
├── P/B < 1: Trading below book value (possible value or distress)
├── P/B 1-2: Fair value range (for mature companies)
├── P/B 2-5: Premium for growth or quality
├── P/B > 5: High growth expectations or intangible value
└── P/B < 0: Negative equity (financial distress)

P/B and ROE Relationship:
A company with high ROE should trade at high P/B
If ROE > Cost of Equity → P/B > 1 (value creation)
If ROE < Cost of Equity → P/B < 1 (value destruction)
If ROE = Cost of Equity → P/B = 1 (fair value)
```

### Price-to-Sales (P/S) Ratio

```
P/S Ratio = Market Cap / Revenue
P/S Ratio = Price per Share / Revenue per Share

When P/S is Useful:
├── Companies with no earnings (turnaround situations)
├── Early-stage growth companies
├── Comparing companies with different profitability
├── Companies with temporarily depressed earnings
└── Revenue is more stable than earnings

Interpretation:
├── P/S < 1: Potentially undervalued (unless low-margin business)
├── P/S 1-3: Fair value for many industries
├── P/S 3-10: Growth premium (SaaS, tech)
├── P/S > 10: Very high growth expectations
└── P/S varies hugely by industry (IT vs Manufacturing)

P/S Limitations:
├── Does not account for profitability
├── Two companies with same P/S can have very different margins
├── Revenue can be recognized aggressively
├── Ignores capital structure
└── Less useful for cyclical companies
```

### EV/EBITDA

```
Enterprise Value (EV) = Market Cap + Debt - Cash + Minority Interest

EV/EBITDA = Enterprise Value / EBITDA

Why EV/EBITDA is Preferred:
├── Capital structure neutral (includes debt)
├── Ignores tax jurisdiction differences
├── Removes D&A differences (depreciation policy)
├── Better for M&A comparisons
├── Common in cross-border comparisons
├── Used by PE firms and investment banks

Interpretation:
├── EV/EBITDA < 6: Potentially undervalued
├── EV/EBITDA 6-10: Fair value for many industries
├── EV/EBITDA 10-15: Growth expectations
├── EV/EBITDA > 15: High growth or overvalued
└── Industry-specific: IT 15-25, Manufacturing 6-10, Banking N/A

Example:
Market Cap: ₹10,000 Cr
Debt: ₹3,000 Cr
Cash: ₹1,000 Cr
Minority Interest: ₹200 Cr
EV = 10,000 + 3,000 - 1,000 + 200 = ₹12,200 Cr

EBITDA: ₹1,500 Cr
EV/EBITDA = 12,200/1,500 = 8.1x
```

## 4.3 Liquidity Ratios

```
Current Ratio = Current Assets / Current Liabilities

Interpretation:
├── > 2: Very liquid (may be inefficient with capital)

Cash Ratio = (Cash + Cash Equivalents + Short-term Investments) / Current Liabilities

Interpretation:
├── > 1: Can pay off all current liabilities with cash alone
├── 0.5-1: Strong cash position
├── 0.3-0.5: Adequate
├── < 0.3: Relies on receivables collection to meet obligations
└── Very high: May indicate excess idle cash
```

## 4.4 Solvency Ratios

```
Debt-to-Equity (D/E) Ratio = Total Debt / Shareholders' Equity

Interpretation:
├── < 0.3: Very low leverage (conservative)
├── 0.3-0.7: Moderate leverage (healthy)
├── 0.7-1.5: High leverage (risky in downturns)
├── > 1.5: Very high leverage (danger zone)
├── > 3: Extreme leverage (potential distress)
└── Industry-specific: Banks naturally have high D/E

Debt-to-Assets Ratio = Total Debt / Total Assets

Interpretation:
├── < 0.3: Low debt burden
├── 0.3-0.5: Moderate
├── 0.5-0.7: High
└── > 0.7: Very high (company financed mostly by debt)

Interest Coverage Ratio = EBIT / Interest Expense

Interpretation:
├── > 10x: Very comfortable (can handle interest many times over)
├── 5-10x: Comfortable
├── 3-5x: Adequate but monitor
├── 1.5-3x: Tight (vulnerable to earnings decline)
├── 1-1.5x: Danger zone (barely covering interest)
└── < 1x: Cannot cover interest from operations (distress)

Financial Leverage = Total Assets / Shareholders' Equity
Financial Leverage = 1 + D/E Ratio

Higher leverage = Higher ROE (when returns exceed cost of debt)
Higher leverage = Higher risk (in downturns, losses are amplified)
```

## 4.5 Efficiency Ratios

```
Asset Turnover = Revenue / Total Assets

Interpretation:
├── > 1.5: Very efficient asset utilization
├── 1-1.5: Good efficiency
├── 0.5-1: Moderate
├── < 0.5: Asset-heavy or underutilized assets
└── Varies by industry (IT >> Banking)

Inventory Turnover = COGS / Average Inventory

Interpretation:

├── Low (2-5x): Slow-moving goods, potential obsolescence risk
├── Very Low (< 2x): Serious inventory management concern

Working Capital Turnover = Revenue / Working Capital
```

# 5. GROWTH ANALYSIS

## 5.1 Revenue Growth

```
Revenue Growth (YoY) = (Current Year Revenue - Previous Year Revenue) / Previous Year Revenue × 100

Organic Growth vs Inorganic Growth:
├── Organic Growth: Growth from existing operations
│   = Total Growth - Acquisition Contribution
│
├── Inorganic Growth: Growth from acquisitions
│   = Revenue from Acquired Companies
│
└── Why it matters:
    15% total growth = 8% organic + 7% inorganic
    Organic growth shows true business momentum
    Inorganic growth is one-time (can't acquire every year)

Revenue Growth Quality Assessment:
├── Sustainable: Growing with or faster than industry
├── Diversified: Growth across products/geographies
├── Profitable: Growth accompanied by margin expansion
├── Cash-backed: Growing revenue converts to growing cash flow
├── Organic: Not dependent on acquisitions
└── Predictable: Consistent growth rate (low variance)
```

## 5.2 Profit Growth

```
Net Profit Growth = (Current PAT - Previous PAT) / Previous PAT × 100

Quality of Profit Growth:
├── Profit growth > Revenue growth: Margin expansion (positive)
├── Profit growth = Revenue growth: Stable margins
├── Profit growth < Revenue growth: Margin compression (negative)
├── Profit growth driven by cost-cutting: Limited sustainability
├── Profit growth driven by revenue growth: More sustainable
└── Profit growth with improving cash conversion: Highest quality

Red Flags in Profit Growth:
├── Profit growing but cash flow declining: Aggressive accounting
├── Profit growth from non-recurring items: Not sustainable
├── Profit growth from tax benefits: One-time
├── Profit growth from lower provisions: Accounting manipulation
└── Profit growth but ROE declining: Equity dilution
```

## 5.3 EPS Growth

```
Basic EPS Growth = (Current Basic EPS - Previous Basic EPS) / Previous Basic EPS × 100
Diluted EPS Growth = (Current Diluted EPS - Previous Diluted EPS) / Previous Diluted EPS × 100

Dilution-Adjusted Analysis:
If Basic EPS grows 20% but Diluted EPS grows only 10%:
→ Significant dilution has occurred (ESOPs, new shares)
→ True shareholder value growth is only 10%

EPS Growth vs Profit Growth:
├── EPS Growth = Profit Growth - Dilution Rate
├── If profit grows 15% but shares increase 5%: EPS grows ~10%
└── Always check share count trend over time
```

## 5.4 EBITDA Growth

```
EBITDA Growth = (Current EBITDA - Previous EBITDA) / Previous EBITDA × 100

Why Track EBITDA Growth:
├── Proxies operating cash flow growth
├── Removes D&A policy differences
├── Common metric for valuation comparison
├── Used in credit analysis
└── Industry-standard for M&A

Better Than EBITDA Growth: Cash EBITDA Growth
Cash EBITDA = EBITDA - CapEx
If EBITDA grows 20% but CapEx grows 30%:
→ Cash EBITDA may be flat or declining
```

## 5.5 Book Value Growth

```
Book Value Growth = (Current BVPS - Previous BVPS) / Previous BVPS × 100

What Drives Book Value Growth:
├── Retained Earnings (PAT - Dividends)
├── Fresh Equity Issuance (increases share capital)
├── Fair Value Adjustments (through OCI)
└── Other Comprehensive Income changes

Significance:
├── Sustained BVPS growth indicates value creation
├── BVPS growth + high ROE = Compounding machine
├── BVPS growth from equity issuance = Dilution (not real growth)
└── BVPS decline = Value destruction or large dividends
```

## 5.6 Free Cash Flow Growth

```
FCF Growth = (Current FCF - Previous FCF) / Previous FCF × 100

Why FCF Growth is Most Important:
├── FCF is actual cash available to shareholders
├── FCF cannot be manipulated (easily)
├── FCF growth drives intrinsic value
├── FCF growth funds dividends, buybacks, acquisitions
└── FCF growth is the ultimate measure of business quality

FCF Yield = FCF / Market Cap × 100
├── FCF Yield > 10%: Potentially undervalued
├── FCF Yield 5-10%: Fairly valued
├── FCF Yield < 5%: Growth expectations or overvalued
└── Compare with bond yields for relative value
```

## 5.7 CAGR Calculation

```
CAGR (Compound Annual Growth Rate) = (End Value / Start Value)^(1/Number of Years) - 1

5-Year Revenue CAGR:
Revenue 5 years ago: ₹1,000 Cr
Current Revenue: ₹2,000 Cr
CAGR = (2,000/1,000)^(1/5) - 1 = 2^(0.2) - 1 = 14.87%

Time Periods and Their Significance:
├── 1-Year Growth: Recent momentum (may be volatile)
├── 3-Year CAGR: Medium-term trend (smooths some volatility)
├── 5-Year CAGR: Established trend (most commonly used)
├── 10-Year CAGR: Long-term track record (rare for growth companies)
└── Use multiple periods for comprehensive view
```

**Python Implementation:**

```python
import numpy as np

def cagr(beginning_value, ending_value, years):
    """Calculate Compound Annual Growth Rate"""
    return (ending_value / beginning_value) ** (1 / years) - 1

def growth_rates(values):
    """Calculate year-over-year growth rates"""
    return [(values[i] - values[i-1]) / values[i-1] * 100 
            for i in range(1, len(values))]

def compound_growth_rate(values, period):
    """Calculate CAGR over a specific period"""
    if len(values) < period + 1:
        return None
    return cagr(values[-(period+1)], values[-1], period)

# Example
revenues = [1000, 1100, 1250, 1400, 1600, 1850]
print(f"1-Year Growth: {growth_rates(revenues)[-1]:.2f}%")
print(f"3-Year CAGR: {compound_growth_rate(revenues, 3):.2f}%")
print(f"5-Year CAGR: {compound_growth_rate(revenues, 5):.2f}%")
```

## 5.8 Sustainable Growth Rate

```
Sustainable Growth Rate (SGR) = ROE × (1 - Dividend Payout Ratio)
SGR = ROE × Retention Ratio

Example:
ROE = 20%
Dividend Payout Ratio = 30%
Retention Ratio = 70%
SGR = 20% × 70% = 14%

Interpretation:
├── Company can grow at 14% without external financing
├── Growth beyond SGR requires: debt, equity, or margin improvement
├── SGR assumes: constant D/E, constant margin, constant asset turnover
└── Violation of SGR assumptions makes it less reliable

SGR vs Actual Growth:
├── Actual < SGR: Company may be under-investing
├── Actual ≈ SGR: Optimal growth trajectory
├── Actual > SGR: Company needs external financing (dilution or debt risk)
└── SGR declining: ROE declining or dividend increasing
```

```

Dividend Payout Ratio = DPS / EPS × 100

Sustainability Levels:

Payout Ratio by Company Type:
├── Growth Companies: 0-20% (reinvesting for growth)
```

```
Dividend Growth Rate = (Current Dividend - Previous Dividend) / Previous Dividend × 100

Dividend CAGR:

Significance:

├── Consistent dividend growth: Rare, indicates quality business
├── 10+ years of dividend growth: "Dividend Aristocrat" status
├── Dividend growth > Inflation: Real income growth
└── Dividend growth in line with profit growth: Sustainable
```

```
Total Debt includes:
```

```
└── Interest cost declining while debt growing: Favorable refinancing
```

# 9. CASH FLOW ANALYSIS

## 9.1 Operating Cash Flow — Quality Assessment

```
OCF Quality Assessment:

Green Flags:
├── OCF consistently > Net Profit (cash conversion > 100%)
├── OCF growing in line with revenue growth
├── OCF positive for 5+ consecutive years
├── OCF covers all CapEx and dividends
├── OCF has low volatility
└── OCF from core operations (not one-time items)

Red Flags:
├── OCF consistently < Net Profit (earnings quality concern)
├── OCF declining while profit increasing (accounting concern)
├── OCF dependent on one-time items
├── OCF negative despite reported profits
├── OCF volatile and unpredictable
└── OCF from financing activities (raising cash to survive)

OCF to Revenue Ratio:
OCF Margin = OCF / Revenue × 100
├── > 20%: Excellent cash generation
├── 15-20%: Good
├── 10-15%: Adequate
├── 5-10%: Low (may indicate working capital issues)
└── < 5%: Poor (serious cash flow concern)
```

## 9.2 Free Cash Flow — Owner Earnings Concept

```
Warren Buffett's Owner Earnings:

Owner Earnings = Net Profit + Depreciation & Amortization - Maintenance CapEx

This represents the true cash available to owners after maintaining
the competitive position of the business.

Example:
Net Profit: ₹500 Cr
D&A: ₹200 Cr
Total CapEx: ₹300 Cr
Maintenance CapEx (estimated): ₹150 Cr
Growth CapEx: ₹150 Cr

Owner Earnings = 500 + 200 - 150 = ₹550 Cr
FCF = 500 + 200 - 300 = ₹400 Cr

The difference (₹150 Cr) is growth investment that creates future value.

Interpretation:
├── Owner Earnings > Reported Profit: Conservative, high quality
├── Owner Earnings < Reported Profit: Maintenance costs eating into profit
├── Owner Earnings growing: Business becoming more valuable
└── Owner Earnings declining: Business may be maturing or deteriorating
```

## 9.3 Cash Conversion Ratio — Detailed

```
Cash Conversion Ratio = Operating Cash Flow / Net Profit

Calculation Example:
Q1: OCF = ₹250 Cr, PAT = ₹200 Cr, CCR = 1.25
Q2: OCF = ₹180 Cr, PAT = ₹220 Cr, CCR = 0.82
Q3: OCF = ₹300 Cr, PAT = ₹250 Cr, CCR = 1.20

├── IT Services: 1.1-1.3 (high cash conversion)

├── Infrastructure: 0.6-0.9 (high working capital needs)
└── Real Estate: Highly variable (advance collections)
```

## 9.4 Operating Cash Flow vs Net Profit — Why They Differ

```
Reasons for Differences:

1. Non-Cash Charges (D&A, Provisions):
   ├── Net Profit is reduced by D&A
   ├── D&A is added back to get OCF
   └── This is the most common difference

2. Working Capital Changes:
   ├── Increase in Receivables: Profit recorded but cash not received
   ├── Increase in Inventory: Cash spent but not yet in COGS
   ├── Increase in Payables: Expense recorded but cash not paid
   └── These timing differences cause OCF ≠ Profit

3. Non-Operating Items:
   ├── Gain on asset sale: Included in profit but not operating cash flow
   ├── Investment income: May be in profit but investing cash flow
   └── Foreign exchange gains/losses: Non-operating

4. Tax Payments:
   ├── Tax expense in P&L may differ from actual tax paid
   ├── Deferred tax creates timing difference
   └── Tax refunds create positive cash flow impact

5. Provisions:
   ├── Provision for bad debts: Reduces profit but no cash outflow
   ├── Actual bad debt write-off: Cash outflow (may be in different period)
   └── Provision reversal: Increases profit but no cash inflow
```

# 13. VALUATION MODELS

## 13.1 Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) — Complete Step-by-Step

```
Step 1: Project Free Cash Flows (5-10 years)

FCF = EBIT(1-Tax Rate) + D&A - CapEx - ΔWorking Capital

Year 1: ₹100 Cr
Year 2: ₹115 Cr (15% growth)
Year 3: ₹132 Cr (15% growth)
Year 4: ₹149 Cr (13% growth)
Year 5: ₹164 Cr (10% growth)

Step 2: Calculate Terminal Value

Method 1: Gordon Growth Model
Terminal Value = FCF_terminal × (1 + g) / (WACC - g)
Where g = perpetual growth rate (typically 3-5%)

Terminal Value = 164 × (1 + 0.04) / (0.12 - 0.04)
Terminal Value = 170.56 / 0.08 = ₹2,132 Cr

Method 2: Exit Multiple
Terminal Value = FCF_terminal × EV/EBITDA multiple
Terminal Value = 164 × 12 = ₹1,968 Cr

Step 3: Calculate WACC (Weighted Average Cost of Capital)

WACC = (E/V × Cost of Equity) + (D/V × Cost of Debt × (1 - Tax Rate))

Cost of Equity (CAPM) = Risk-Free Rate + Beta × Market Risk Premium
= 7% + 1.2 × 6% = 14.2%

Cost of Debt (after tax) = Interest Rate × (1 - Tax Rate)
= 8% × (1 - 0.25) = 6%

WACC = (0.7 × 14.2%) + (0.3 × 6%)
WACC = 9.94% + 1.8% = 11.74%
≈ 12% (rounded)

Step 4: Discount Cash Flows

PV = FCF / (1 + WACC)^n

Year 1: 100 / 1.12^1 = ₹89.29 Cr
Year 2: 115 / 1.12^2 = ₹91.71 Cr
Year 3: 132 / 1.12^3 = ₹93.96 Cr
Year 4: 149 / 1.12^4 = ₹94.68 Cr
Year 5: 164 / 1.12^5 = ₹93.09 Cr

PV of Terminal Value: 2,132 / 1.12^5 = ₹1,210.07 Cr

Step 5: Calculate Enterprise Value

EV = Sum of PV of FCFs + PV of Terminal Value
EV = (89.29 + 91.71 + 93.96 + 94.68 + 93.09) + 1,210.07
EV = 462.73 + 1,210.07 = ₹1,672.80 Cr

Step 6: Calculate Equity Value

Equity Value = Enterprise Value - Debt + Cash
Equity Value = 1,672.80 - 500 + 200 = ₹1,372.80 Cr

Step 7: Calculate Intrinsic Value Per Share

Intrinsic Value = Equity Value / Number of Shares
Intrinsic Value = 1,372.80 / 100 = ₹13.73 per share

Step 8: Apply Margin of Safety

Margin of Safety = 25-30%
Buy Price = ₹13.73 × (1 - 0.25) = ₹10.30 per share

Step 9: Sensitivity Analysis

WACC \ Growth Rate | 2%    | 3%    | 4%    | 5%
10%                 | ₹14.2 | ₹16.8 | ₹20.5 | ₹26.3
11%                 | ₹12.1 | ₹14.0 | ₹16.6 | ₹20.3
12%                 | ₹10.5 | ₹11.9 | ₹13.8 | ₹16.5
13%                 | ₹9.2  | ₹10.3 | ₹11.7 | ₹13.6
14%                 | ₹8.1  | ₹9.0  | ₹10.1 | ₹11.5

Shows: Small changes in WACC or growth rate significantly impact valuation
```

**Python DCF Implementation:**

```python
import numpy as np

def dcf_valuation(fcf_projections, terminal_growth_rate, wacc, 
                   debt, cash, shares_outstanding, margin_of_safety=0.25):
    """
    Complete DCF Valuation Model
    
    Parameters:
    -----------
    fcf_projections : list
        Projected free cash flows for years 1-N
    terminal_growth_rate : float
        Perpetual growth rate (e.g., 0.03 for 3%)
    wacc : float
        Weighted Average Cost of Capital (e.g., 0.12 for 12%)
    debt : float
        Total debt
    cash : float
        Cash and equivalents
    shares_outstanding : float
        Number of shares
    margin_of_safety : float
        Margin of safety (e.g., 0.25 for 25%)
    
    Returns:
    --------
    dict with enterprise_value, equity_value, intrinsic_value, buy_price
    """
    # Step 1: Calculate PV of projected FCFs
    years = np.arange(1, len(fcf_projections) + 1)
    pv_fcfs = fcf_projections / (1 + wacc) ** years
    
    # Step 2: Calculate Terminal Value (Gordon Growth Model)
    terminal_fcf = fcf_projections[-1] * (1 + terminal_growth_rate)
    terminal_value = terminal_fcf / (wacc - terminal_growth_rate)
    pv_terminal = terminal_value / (1 + wacc) ** len(fcf_projections)
    
    # Step 3: Enterprise Value
    enterprise_value = np.sum(pv_fcfs) + pv_terminal
    
    # Step 4: Equity Value
    equity_value = enterprise_value - debt + cash
    
    # Step 5: Intrinsic Value Per Share
    intrinsic_value = equity_value / shares_outstanding
    
    # Step 6: Buy Price with Margin of Safety
    buy_price = intrinsic_value * (1 - margin_of_safety)
    
    return {
        'pv_fcfs': np.sum(pv_fcfs),
        'pv_terminal': pv_terminal,
        'terminal_value': terminal_value,
        'enterprise_value': enterprise_value,
        'equity_value': equity_value,
        'intrinsic_value_per_share': intrinsic_value,
        'buy_price': buy_price,
        'margin_of_safety_applied': margin_of_safety
    }

# Example Usage
fcf_projections = np.array([100, 115, 132, 149, 164])  # in ₹ Cr
result = dcf_valuation(
    fcf_projections=fcf_projections,
    terminal_growth_rate=0.04,
    wacc=0.12,
    debt=500,
    cash=200,
    shares_outstanding=100,
    margin_of_safety=0.25
)

for key, value in result.items():
    print(f"{key}: ₹{value:.2f} Cr" if key != 'margin_of_safety_applied' 
          else f"{key}: {value:.0%}")
```

## 13.2 Dividend Discount Model (DDM)

```
Gordon Growth Model (Constant Growth DDM):

Intrinsic Value = D₁ / (k - g)
Where:
D₁ = Expected dividend next year
k = Required rate of return
g = Constant dividend growth rate

Example:
Current Dividend: ₹5
Expected Dividend Growth: 8%
Required Rate of Return: 15%
D₁ = 5 × (1 + 0.08) = ₹5.40

Intrinsic Value = 5.40 / (0.15 - 0.08) = ₹77.14

Two-Stage DDM (for high growth + stable growth):

Stage 1 (High Growth, 5 years):
Year 1: ₹5.40 (8% growth)
Year 2: ₹5.83 (8% growth)
Year 3: ₹6.30 (8% growth)
Year 4: ₹6.80 (8% growth)
Year 5: ₹7.35 (8% growth)

Stage 2 (Stable Growth, perpetual):
Terminal Value = D₆ / (k - g₂)
D₆ = 7.35 × (1 + 0.04) = ₹7.64
Terminal Value = 7.64 / (0.15 - 0.04) = ₹69.45

PV of Stage 1 Dividends:
Year 1: 5.40/1.15 = 4.70
Year 2: 5.83/1.15² = 4.40
Year 3: 6.30/1.15³ = 4.14
Year 4: 6.80/1.15⁴ = 3.90
Year 5: 7.35/1.15⁵ = 3.65
Total PV of dividends: ₹20.79

PV of Terminal Value: 69.45/1.15⁵ = ₹34.56

Intrinsic Value = 20.79 + 34.56 = ₹55.35

When DDM is Applicable:
├── Company pays regular dividends
├── Dividend growth is predictable
├── Company is mature and stable
├── Dividend payout is consistent
└── Not suitable for growth companies not paying dividends
```

## 13.4 Intrinsic Value Calculation

```
Intrinsic Value Framework:

Approach 1: DCF-Based
Intrinsic Value = PV of future free cash flows

Approach 2: Earnings-Based
Intrinsic Value = Normalized EPS × Fair PE Multiple

Approach 3: Asset-Based
Intrinsic Value = Net Asset Value per share

Approach 4: Dividend-Based
Intrinsic Value = Present value of future dividends

Combining Approaches:
├── Calculate intrinsic value using each method
├── Assign weights based on suitability
│   ├── DCF: 40% (most theoretically correct)
│   ├── Relative: 30% (market-based check)
│   ├── Earnings: 20% (earnings power value)
│   └── Asset: 10% (floor value)
├── Weighted average = Estimated intrinsic value
└── Apply margin of safety for buy price

Normalized Earnings:
├── Remove one-time items (gains, losses)
├── Average earnings over a full cycle (5-7 years)
├── Adjust for current capacity/utilization
├── Adjust for current tax rates
└── Use sustainable earnings power
```

## 13.5 Margin of Safety (Benjamin Graham Concept)

```
Margin of Safety = (Intrinsic Value - Current Price) / Intrinsic Value × 100

Or equivalently:
Buy Price = Intrinsic Value × (1 - Margin of Safety)

Recommended Margins:
├── Blue Chip / Large Cap: 15-20%
├── Mid Cap / Quality Growth: 20-30%
├── Small Cap / Turnaround: 30-40%
├── Cyclical / Commodity: 30-40%
└── Distressed / Special Situation: 40-50%

Example:
Intrinsic Value (DCF): ₹500 per share
Current Market Price: ₹350 per share
Margin of Safety = (500 - 350) / 500 = 30%

Analysis:
├── 30% margin of safety provides cushion against:
│   ├── Estimation errors in DCF
│   ├── Unexpected business deterioration
│   ├── Market downturn
│   ├── Changes in competitive landscape
│   └── Execution risk
├── If intrinsic value is actually ₹400 (not ₹500):
│   Still buying at ₹350 (12.5% discount to actual value)
└── The margin of safety protects against being wrong

Graham's Original Framework:
├── Buy at least 30% below intrinsic value
├── Diversify across 20-30 stocks
├── Use conservative estimates
├── Ensure adequate size (market cap)
└── Ensure financial strength (low debt, good ratios)
```

## 13.6 Reverse DCF

```
Reverse DCF asks: What growth rate is the market pricing in?

Steps:
1. Calculate WACC (same as regular DCF)
2. Use current market price as "intrinsic value"
3. Solve for implied growth rate

Formula:
Market Cap = FCF₁ / (WACC - g_implied)
g_implied = WACC - FCF₁ / Market Cap

Example:
Current Market Cap: ₹10,000 Cr
FCF next year: ₹800 Cr
WACC: 12%

g_implied = 0.12 - 800/10,000 = 0.12 - 0.08 = 4%

Interpretation:
├── Market is pricing in 4% perpetual FCF growth
├── Is this realistic for the company?
├── If company can grow faster than 4%: Potentially undervalued
├── If company can only grow 2-3%: Potentially overvalued
└── Compare implied growth with historical and industry growth
```

## 14.1 Piotroski F-Score (All 9 Criteria)

The Piotroski F-Score is a 0-9 score measuring financial strength. Score of 8-9 = strong, 0-2 = weak.

```
PROFITABILITY (4 Criteria):

1. Return on Assets (ROA)
   ├── Score 1: ROA > 0 (company is profitable)
   └── Score 0: ROA ≤ 0

2. Operating Cash Flow (OCF)
   ├── Score 1: OCF > 0 (generating cash from operations)
   └── Score 0: OCF ≤ 0

3. Change in ROA (ΔROA)
   ├── Score 1: Current ROA > Previous ROA (improving profitability)
   └── Score 0: Current ROA ≤ Previous ROA

4. Accruals (OCF vs Net Income)
   ├── Score 1: OCF > Net Income (cash quality of earnings)
   └── Score 0: OCF ≤ Net Income

LEVERAGE/LIQUIDITY (3 Criteria):

5. Change in Leverage (Long-term Debt / Average Assets)
   ├── Score 1: Current ratio < Previous ratio (decreasing leverage)
   │   Change = Current LTD/Avg Assets - Previous LTD/Avg Assets
   │   Score 1: Change < 0 (leverage decreased)
   └── Score 0: Change ≥ 0

6. Change in Liquidity (Current Ratio)
   ├── Score 1: Current Ratio increased (improving liquidity)
   └── Score 0: Current Ratio decreased or stayed same

7. Equity Dilution (Shares Outstanding)
   ├── Score 1: No new shares issued (no dilution)
   └── Score 0: New shares issued (dilution occurred)

OPERATING EFFICIENCY (2 Criteria):

8. Change in Gross Margin
   ├── Score 1: Gross Margin increased (improving efficiency/pricing)
   └── Score 0: Gross Margin decreased

9. Change in Asset Turnover
   ├── Score 1: Asset Turnover increased (improving efficiency)
   └── Score 0: Asset Turnover decreased

SCORING:
├── 8-9: Strong buy candidate (strong fundamentals)
├── 7: Good (solid fundamentals)
├── 5-6: Neutral (average fundamentals)
├── 3-4: Weak (concerning fundamentals)
├── 0-2: Avoid (weak fundamentals, potential value trap)
└── F-Score works best as a screening tool, not sole criterion
```

**Python Implementation:**

```python
def piotroski_f_score(financials):
    """
    Calculate Piotroski F-Score
    
    Parameters:
    -----------
    financials : dict
        Dictionary with financial metrics for current and previous year
    
    Returns:
    --------
    int : F-Score (0-9)
    dict : Breakdown of each criterion
    """
    score = 0
    breakdown = {}
    
    # PROFITABILITY (4 criteria)
    
    # 1. ROA > 0
    roa = financials['net_profit'] / financials['total_assets']
    criterion_1 = 1 if roa > 0 else 0
    score += criterion_1
    breakdown['1_ROA_positive'] = criterion_1
    
    # 2. Operating Cash Flow > 0
    ocf = financials['operating_cash_flow']
    criterion_2 = 1 if ocf > 0 else 0
    score += criterion_2
    breakdown['2_OCF_positive'] = criterion_2
    
    # 3. Change in ROA (current > previous)
    roa_current = financials['net_profit'] / financials['total_assets']
    roa_previous = financials['net_profit_prev'] / financials['total_assets_prev']
    criterion_3 = 1 if roa_current > roa_previous else 0
    score += criterion_3
    breakdown['3_ROA_improving'] = criterion_3
    
    # 4. Accruals (OCF > Net Income)
    criterion_4 = 1 if ocf > financials['net_profit'] else 0
    score += criterion_4
    breakdown['4_Accruals'] = criterion_4
    
    # LEVERAGE/LIQUIDITY (3 criteria)
    
    # 5. Change in Leverage (decreasing = good)
    leverage_current = financials['long_term_debt'] / financials['total_assets']
    leverage_prev = financials['long_term_debt_prev'] / financials['total_assets_prev']
    criterion_5 = 1 if leverage_current < leverage_prev else 0
    score += criterion_5
    breakdown['5_Leverage_decreasing'] = criterion_5
    
    # 6. Change in Current Ratio (increasing = good)
    cr_current = financials['current_assets'] / financials['current_liabilities']
    cr_prev = financials['current_assets_prev'] / financials['current_liabilities_prev']
    criterion_6 = 1 if cr_current > cr_prev else 0
    score += criterion_6
    breakdown['6_Liquidity_improving'] = criterion_6
    
    # 7. No equity dilution
    criterion_7 = 1 if financials['shares_outstanding'] <= financials['shares_outstanding_prev'] else 0
    score += criterion_7
    breakdown['7_No_dilution'] = criterion_7
    
    # OPERATING EFFICIENCY (2 criteria)
    
    # 8. Gross Margin improving
    gm_current = financials['gross_profit'] / financials['revenue']
    gm_prev = financials['gross_profit_prev'] / financials['revenue_prev']
    criterion_8 = 1 if gm_current > gm_prev else 0
    score += criterion_8
    breakdown['8_Gross_margin_improving'] = criterion_8
    
    # 9. Asset Turnover improving
    at_current = financials['revenue'] / financials['total_assets']
    at_prev = financials['revenue_prev'] / financials['total_assets_prev']
    criterion_9 = 1 if at_current > at_prev else 0
    score += criterion_9
    breakdown['9_Asset_turnover_improving'] = criterion_9
    
    return score, breakdown

# Example
financials = {
    'net_profit': 500, 'total_assets': 5000,
    'net_profit_prev': 450, 'total_assets_prev': 4800,
    'operating_cash_flow': 600,
    'long_term_debt': 1000, 'long_term_debt_prev': 1200,
    'current_assets': 2000, 'current_liabilities': 1500,
    'current_assets_prev': 1800, 'current_liabilities_prev': 1400,
    'shares_outstanding': 100, 'shares_outstanding_prev': 100,
    'gross_profit': 1500, 'gross_profit_prev': 1300,
    'revenue': 5000, 'revenue_prev': 4500,
}

score, breakdown = piotroski_f_score(financials)
print(f"Piotroski F-Score: {score}/9")
for criterion, result in breakdown.items():
    print(f"  {criterion}: {'✓' if result else '✗'}")
```

## 14.2 Altman Z-Score

```
Original Z-Score (Manufacturing Companies):

Z = 1.2A + 1.4B + 3.3C + 0.6D + 1.0E

Where:
A = Working Capital / Total Assets
B = Retained Earnings / Total Assets
C = EBIT / Total Assets
D = Market Value of Equity / Total Liabilities
E = Revenue / Total Assets

Interpretation:
├── Z > 2.99: "Safe Zone" — Low probability of bankruptcy
├── 1.81 < Z < 2.99: "Grey Zone" — Uncertain, needs monitoring
└── Z < 1.81: "Distress Zone" — High probability of bankruptcy

Modified Z-Score (Non-Manufacturing / All Companies):

Z' = 0.717A + 0.847B + 3.107C + 0.420D + 0.998E

Example Calculation:
Total Assets: ₹10,000 Cr
Working Capital: ₹2,000 Cr
Retained Earnings: ₹3,000 Cr
EBIT: ₹1,500 Cr
Market Cap: ₹8,000 Cr
Total Liabilities: ₹5,000 Cr
Revenue: ₹12,000 Cr

A = 2,000/10,000 = 0.20
B = 3,000/10,000 = 0.30
C = 1,500/10,000 = 0.15
D = 8,000/5,000 = 1.60
E = 12,000/10,000 = 1.20

Z = 1.2(0.20) + 1.4(0.30) + 3.3(0.15) + 0.6(1.60) + 1.0(1.20)
Z = 0.24 + 0.42 + 0.495 + 0.96 + 1.20
Z = 3.315

Result: SAFE ZONE (Z > 2.99)
```

**Python Implementation:**

```python
def altman_z_score(total_assets, working_capital, retained_earnings,
                   ebit, market_cap_equity, total_liabilities, revenue):
    """
    Calculate Altman Z-Score
    
    Returns: (z_score, zone, interpretation)
    """
    A = working_capital / total_assets
    B = retained_earnings / total_assets
    C = ebit / total_assets
    D = market_cap_equity / total_liabilities
    E = revenue / total_assets
    
    z = 1.2 * A + 1.4 * B + 3.3 * C + 0.6 * D + 1.0 * E
    
    if z > 2.99:
        zone = "Safe Zone"
        interpretation = "Low bankruptcy probability"
    elif z > 1.81:
        zone = "Grey Zone"
        interpretation = "Uncertain - monitor closely"
    else:
        zone = "Distress Zone"
        interpretation = "High bankruptcy probability"
    
    return z, zone, interpretation

# Example

    total_assets=10000,
    working_capital=2000,
    retained_earnings=3000,
    ebit=1500,
    market_cap_equity=8000,
    total_liabilities=5000,
    revenue=12000
)
print(f"Z-Score: {z:.3f} | Zone: {zone} | {interp}")
```

## 14.3 Beneish M-Score (Earnings Manipulation Detection)

```
M = -4.84 + 0.920(DSRI) + 0.528(GMI) + 0.404(AQI) + 0.892(SGI)
```

## 14.4 Graham Number

```
Graham Number = √(22.5 × EPS × BVPS)

Where:
├── 22.5 = 15 (maximum PE Graham considered) × 1.5 (maximum PB Graham considered)
├── EPS = Earnings Per Share (trailing 12 months)
└── BVPS = Book Value Per Share

Example:
EPS: ₹25
BVPS: ₹150

Graham Number = √(22.5 × 25 × 150) = √(84,375) = ₹290.47

Interpretation:
├── If Current Price < Graham Number: Potentially undervalued
├── If Current Price > Graham Number: Potentially overvalued
├── Margin of Safety: Buy at 20-30% below Graham Number
└── Buy Price = 290.47 × 0.75 = ₹217.85

Limitations:
├── Very conservative (may never trigger for quality companies)
├── Assumes maximum PE of 15 and PB of 1.5
├── Not suitable for growth companies
├── Works best for value/contrarian investing
└── Should be used alongside other metrics, not alone
```

## 14.5 Magic Formula (Joel Greenblatt)

```
Magic Formula Ranking:

Step 1: Calculate Earnings Yield
Earnings Yield = EBIT / Enterprise Value

Step 2: Calculate Return on Invested Capital (ROIC)
ROIC = EBIT / Invested Capital
Invested Capital = Net Working Capital + Net Fixed Assets

Step 3: Rank All Companies
├── Rank by Earnings Yield (highest = Rank 1)
├── Rank by ROIC (highest = Rank 1)
├── Combined Rank = Earnings Yield Rank + ROIC Rank
└── Sort by Combined Rank (lowest combined = best)

Example:
| Company    | Earnings Yield | EY Rank | ROIC  | ROIC Rank | Combined |
|-----------|---------------|---------|-------|-----------|----------|
| Company A | 18%           | 1       | 35%   | 2         | 3        |
| Company B | 15%           | 2       | 30%   | 3         | 5        |
| Company C | 12%           | 3       | 40%   | 1         | 4        |
| Company D | 10%           | 4       | 25%   | 4         | 8        |
| Company E | 8%            | 5       | 20%   | 5         | 10       |

Best picks: Companies A (combined rank 3) and C (combined rank 4)

Key Points:
├── Buy top 20-30 companies by combined rank
├── Hold for 1 year, then rebalance
├── Diversify across sectors
├── Avoid financial companies (different metrics)
├── Requires patience (1-year holding period)
└── Historically outperformed market by 15%+ annually
```

## 14.6 Earnings Quality Assessment

```
Earnings Quality Checklist:

1. Cash Flow Check:
   ├── OCF / Net Profit > 1.0: High quality
   ├── OCF / Net Profit 0.7-1.0: Acceptable
   ├── OCF / Net Profit < 0.7: Low quality
   └── OCF negative while profit positive: Very low quality

2. Accruals Check:
   ├── Low accruals (OCF ≈ Net Profit): High quality
   ├── High accruals (OCF < Net Profit): Low quality
   └── Negative accruals (OCF > Net Profit): High quality

3. Revenue Quality:
   ├── Recurring revenue > 50%: High quality
   ├── One-time revenue > 20%: Low quality
   ├── Related party revenue > 10%: Investigate
   └── Revenue recognition changes: Red flag

4. Expense Management:
   ├── Provisions declining while profit rising: May be manipulated
   ├── Depreciation policy changes: Investigate
   ├── One-time gains masking operating losses: Red flag
   └── Cost cutting without revenue decline: Sustainable only if structural

5. Balance Sheet Quality:
   ├── Receivable days increasing: Revenue may not be real
   ├── Inventory days increasing: Demand may be slowing
   ├── CWIP growing without corresponding CapEx: Capitalize expenses
   └── Goodwill increasing: Acquisition-driven growth (check goodwill impairment)
```

```
├── Auto: Higher rates → Lower EMI → Lower sales
├── Capital Goods: Higher rates → Lower CapEx → Lower orders
├── FMCG: Minimal direct impact (pricing power)
└── IT: Minimal direct impact (USD-denominated revenue)

Interest Rate Cycle:
├── Rising Rate Phase: Banks outperform, growth stocks underperform
├── Peak Rate Phase: Bond yields peak, equity may bottom
├── Falling Rate Phase: Growth stocks outperform, banks may underperform
├── Trough Rate Phase: Bond yields bottom, equity may top
└── Duration Risk: Long-term bonds most sensitive to rate changes
```

```
RBI Repo Rate → Bank MCLR → Loan Rates → Consumer/Business Impact

Transmission Mechanism:
├── Repo rate cut: Banks reduce lending rates → More borrowing → Economic growth
├── Repo rate hike: Banks increase lending rates → Less borrowing → Slowdown
├── Transmission lag: 3-6 months for full impact
└── Variable rate loans: Immediate impact, fixed rate: At reset

Impact on Sectors:
├── Housing Finance: Direct impact on EMI and demand
├── Auto Finance: Impact on vehicle financing costs
├── Corporate Lending: Impact on corporate profitability
├── Bond Markets: Inverse relationship with bond prices
├── Deposit Rates: Impact on bank funding costs
└── Equity Markets: Rate cuts generally positive, hikes negative
```

## 16.6 Monetary Policy

```

Liquidity Management:
├── CRR (Cash Reserve Ratio): % of deposits banks keep with RBI

├── SLR (Statutory Liquidity Ratio): % in government securities
│   Higher SLR → Less lending capacity → Higher rates

├── Liquidity surplus → Bond yields fall → Bond prices rise
├── Liquidity deficit → Bond yields rise → Bond prices fall
├── Rate cuts → Equity markets generally rise
├── Rate hikes → Equity markets generally fall
└── Liquidity crunch → Credit growth slows → Economic slowdown
```

# 19. INSIDER ACTIVITY

## 19.1 Insider Buying

```
Insider Buying Analysis:

What Constitutes Insider Buying:
├── Promoter buying from open market
├── CEO/CFO/Directors buying shares
├── Company buying back its own shares
├── Conversion of convertible instruments by insiders
└── Exercise of stock options (and holding, not selling)

Strong Insider Buying Signals:
├── Cluster buying (multiple insiders buying simultaneously)
├── CEO/CFO buying (highest conviction signal)
├── Buying at 52-week lows (contrarian confidence)
├── Large purchases relative to insider's salary/wealth
├── Buying after a period of no insider activity
├── Buying during market panic/correction
└── Insider buying accompanied by positive fundamental developments

Weak Insider Buying Signals:
├── Small purchases (token amounts)
├── Routine/automatic purchases (pre-planned)
├── Buying in companies where insiders must maintain minimum holdings
└── Buying after stock has already risen significantly

Disclosure Requirements (SEBI):
├── insiders must disclose within 2 trading days of transaction
├── Published on BSE/NSE website
├── Quarterly disclosure of all insider trades
└── Restriction period: Cannot trade 30 days before results
```

## 19.2 Insider Selling

```
Insider Selling Analysis:

Planned vs Unplanned Selling:
├── Planned (10b5-1 Plan / Pre-scheduled):
│   ├── Set up in advance, automatic execution
│   ├── Usually for diversification, tax planning
│   ├── Generally not a negative signal
│   └── Check if plan was modified recently

├── Unplanned/Unusual Selling:

│   ├── Multiple insiders selling = investigate
│   ├── Selling before results = potential red flag
│   ├── Selling concentrated in short period = concern
│   └── Selling large portion of holdings = very concerning
│
└── Normal Profit Booking:
    ├── Selling after significant stock price appreciation
    ├── Selling to meet personal financial needs
    ├── Selling for diversification
    └── Selling after long period of no selling

Red Flags:
├── CEO/CFO selling large amounts
├── Multiple insiders selling simultaneously
├── Selling before negative news/results
├── Selling after stock has declined significantly (panic selling by insiders)
├── Selling more than 50% of holdings
├── Selling in company with declining fundamentals
└── Selling after giving positive guidance (hyping then selling)
```

## 19.3 Share Buyback

```
Share Buyback Analysis:

Types of Buybacks:
├── Open Market Purchase:
│   ├── Company buys shares from open market
│   ├── Most common in India
│   ├── Price impact depends on size relative to daily volume
│   └── Flexible timing
│
├── Tender Offer:
│   ├── Company offers to buy shares at fixed price (premium to market)
│   ├── shareholders decide whether to tender
│   ├── More impactful signal (management believes stock undervalued)
│   └── Usually at 15-25% premium to market price
│
└── Book Building:
    ├── Company invites offers from shareholders
    ├── Price determined through bidding process
    └── Less common in India

Buyback Signaling Effect:
├── Management believes stock is undervalued
├── Company has excess cash with no better use
├── Tax-efficient way to return cash (vs dividends)
├── Improves EPS by reducing share count
├── Signals confidence in future cash flows
└── Can support stock price during market weakness

Buyback Impact Assessment:
├── Buyback Yield = Buyback Amount / Market Cap
│   ├── > 5%: Significant impact on EPS
│   ├── 2-5%: Moderate impact
│   ├── < 2%: Minimal impact
│   └── > 10%: Very large, rare
│
├── EPS Accretion = (Post-Buyback EPS - Pre-Buyback EPS) / Pre-Buyback EPS
│   Depends on: Price paid, number of shares bought, financing cost
│
└── Execution Assessment:
    ├── Has company actually completed the buyback?
    ├── How much of announced buyback was executed?
    ├── What price was paid (vs market price)?
    └── What is the timeline for execution?
```

## 19.4 ESOP Issuance

```
ESOP (Employee Stock Option Plan) Analysis:

ESOP Structure:
├── Grant Date: When options are given to employees
├── Vesting Period: Time before options can be exercised (typically 1-4 years)
├── Exercise Date: When employee converts options to shares
├── Exercise Price: Price at which employee can buy shares
├── Expiry Date: When unexercised options lapse
└── Strike Price: Usually at market price on grant date

Dilution Impact:
├── Total ESOP pool as % of outstanding shares
│   ├── < 1%: Minimal dilution
│   ├── 1-3%: Moderate dilution
│   ├── 3-5%: Significant dilution
│   └── > 5%: Very high dilution (concerning)
│
├── Annual ESOP exercises as % of shares
│   ├── Track how many options are being exercised each year
│   └── Calculate dilution impact on EPS
│
└── Vesting schedule impact
    ├── Large vesting in single year = spike in dilution
    ├── Staggered vesting = smoother dilution
    └── Check upcoming vesting dates

Red Flags:
├── ESOPs granted at significant discount to market price
├── ESOPs given to promoters' family members
├── Large ESOP pool without performance linkage
├── ESOPs exercised and immediately sold (insiders don't believe in stock)
├── ESOP costs not properly disclosed
└── Performance conditions easily achievable
```

# 20. CORPORATE ACTIONS

## 20.1 Stock Split

```
Stock Split Analysis:

What is a Stock Split?
├── Company divides each share into multiple shares
├── Face value reduces proportionally
├── Number of shares increases proportionally
├── Market cap remains unchanged
├── No change in fundamentals
└── Example: 1:2 split — ₹10 face value becomes ₹5, number doubles

Impact Analysis:
├── Liquidity Impact: More shares, more trading, better liquidity
├── Psychological Impact: Lower price looks "cheaper" (retail attraction)
├── Index Impact: May affect index weightage (free-float calculation)
├── Price Impact: Short-term positive (increased liquidity)
└── Long-term Impact: Depends on fundamentals, not split itself

Split vs No-Split Assessment:
├── Companies with high share price may split to improve liquidity
├── Companies above ₹10,000 per share may consider splitting
├── Splits are generally positive signals (management expects continued growth)
└── Check if competitors have split (industry trend)
```

## 20.2 Bonus Issue

```
Bonus Issue Analysis:

What is a Bonus Issue?
├── Company issues free shares to existing shareholders
├── Funded from reserves and surplus
├── Face value remains same
├── Number of shares increases
├── Price adjusts proportionally
├── Market cap remains unchanged
└── Example: 1:1 bonus — 1 share for every 1 held, price halves

Bonus Issue vs Stock Split:
├── Bonus: Funded from reserves (converts reserves to share capital)
├── Split: Only changes face value (no accounting entry)
├── Both increase liquidity and reduce price
├── Bonus may signal management's confidence in future earnings
└── Split is purely technical (no financial implication)

Key Questions:
├── Is the bonus issue supported by strong reserves?
├── Is the company generating enough profit to justify?
├── What is the payout ratio after bonus?
├── Is the bonus issue accompanied by strong results?
└── What is management's track record with corporate actions?
```

## 20.3 Rights Issue

```
Rights Issue Analysis:

What is a Rights Issue?
├── Company offers new shares to existing shareholders
├── Offered at discount to market price
├── Shareholders can buy in proportion to holdings
├── Shareholders can sell rights (Renounceable)
├── Dilutive if shareholders don't exercise
└── Typically priced at 10-25% discount to market

Rights Issue Assessment:
├── Why is company raising money?
│   ├── Growth CapEx: Positive if returns > cost of capital
│   ├── Debt reduction: Positive if reducing leverage
│   ├── Working capital: Neutral to positive
│   ├── Acquisition: Depends on acquisition quality
│   └── Covering losses: Red flag (financial distress)
│
├── Pricing:
│   ├── Discount to market: Normal, attractive for subscribers
│   ├── Premium to market: Unusual, may indicate desperation
│   └── At market price: Neutral
│
├── Usage of Proceeds:
│   ├── Clearly stated and reasonable: Positive
│   ├── Vague or general: Concerning
│   └── Not disclosed: Red flag
│
└── Dilution Impact:
    ├── Pre-rights EPS vs Post-rights EPS
    ├── Book value dilution
    └── Impact on ROE
```

## 20.4 Dividend Types and Policies

```
Types of Dividends:

Interim Dividend:
├── Paid during the financial year (quarterly/half-yearly)

├── May be more frequent but smaller amounts
├── Signals regular cash generation
└── Check consistency and growth trend

Final Dividend:
├── Paid after year-end results
├── Declared at AGM
├── Usually larger than interim
├── Based on full-year performance
└── Check if growing in line with profits

Special Dividend:

├── Usually from asset sale, extraordinary gain
├── Should not be capitalized into recurring yield
├── Check source of special dividend
└── May signal management has no better use of cash

Stock Dividend:
├── Not common in India
├── Instead of cash, shareholders receive shares
├── Similar to bonus issue but different accounting
└── Rare in Indian markets

Dividend Policy Assessment:
├── Policy clarity: Is there a stated dividend policy?
├── Payout ratio target: What % of profits does company aim to pay?
├── Growth commitment: Does company commit to growing dividends?
├── Floor dividend: Is there a minimum dividend commitment?
└── Flexibility: Can company cut dividend in tough times without backlash?
```

# 21. ADVANCED FUNDAMENTAL METRICS

## 21.1 Economic Value Added (EVA)

```
EVA = NOPAT - (Invested Capital × WACC)

Where:
├── NOPAT = Net Operating Profit After Tax = EBIT × (1 - Tax Rate)
├── Invested Capital = Total Assets - Current Liabilities = Equity + Debt
├── WACC = Weighted Average Cost of Capital
└── Capital Charge = Invested Capital × WACC

Example:
EBIT: ₹1,500 Cr
Tax Rate: 25%
NOPAT = 1,500 × (1 - 0.25) = ₹1,125 Cr

Invested Capital: ₹8,000 Cr
WACC: 12%
Capital Charge = 8,000 × 0.12 = ₹960 Cr

EVA = 1,125 - 960 = ₹165 Cr

Interpretation:
├── EVA > 0: Company creating value above cost of capital (positive)
├── EVA = 0: Company earning exactly its cost of capital (neutral)
├── EVA < 0: Company destroying value (negative)
├── EVA increasing: Value creation improving
└── EVA declining: Value creation deteriorating

EVA vs Traditional Metrics:
├── ROE doesn't account for cost of equity
├── EVA accounts for ALL capital costs (debt + equity)
├── EVA is in absolute terms (can add across years)
├── EVA motivates management to create shareholder value
└── EVA used by consulting firms (Stern Stewart) for performance measurement
```

## 21.2 Return on Invested Capital (ROIC)

```
ROIC = NOPAT / Invested Capital × 100

Where:
├── NOPAT = EBIT × (1 - Tax Rate)
├── Invested Capital = Total Assets - Non-Interest-Bearing Current Liabilities
│   = Net Working Capital + Net Fixed Assets
│   = Equity + Interest-Bearing Debt
└── Alternative: Invested Capital = Book Value of Equity + Book Value of Debt - Cash

Detailed Calculation:
EBIT: ₹1,500 Cr
Tax Rate: 25%
NOPAT = 1,500 × 0.75 = ₹1,125 Cr

Total Assets: ₹10,000 Cr
Non-Interest Current Liabilities: ₹2,000 Cr
Invested Capital = 10,000 - 2,000 = ₹8,000 Cr

ROIC = 1,125 / 8,000 × 100 = 14.06%

ROIC vs ROE vs ROCE:
├── ROE = Net Profit / Equity (leverage-dependent)
├── ROCE = EBIT / Capital Employed (includes depreciation in capital)
├── ROIC = NOPAT / Invested Capital (most accurate measure of returns)
└── ROIC is the gold standard for value creation analysis

Key Rule:
If ROIC > WACC: Company creating value
If ROIC < WACC: Company destroying value
If ROIC = WACC: Company breaking even on capital

Example:
ROIC = 14.06%
WACC = 12%
Value Spread = 14.06% - 12% = 2.06% (positive → value creation)
```

## 21.3 Enterprise Value (EV)

```
Enterprise Value = Market Cap + Debt - Cash + Minority Interest + Preferred Shares

Components:
├── Market Cap: Equity value (share price × shares)
├── Total Debt: All interest-bearing liabilities
│   ├── Short-term debt
│   ├── Long-term debt
│   ├── Lease liabilities (Ind AS 116)
│   └── Debentures/Bonds
├── Cash & Equivalents: All liquid assets
│   ├── Cash in bank
│   ├── Short-term investments
│   └── Marketable securities
├── Minority Interest: Non-controlling shareholders' share
└── Preferred Shares: Value of preferred equity

Adjustments:
├── Operating leases: Add back if using EV (lease liabilities)
├── Joint venture equity: Add proportionate share
├── Pension obligations: Add underfunded pension liability
├── Environmental provisions: Add estimated cleanup costs
└── Off-balance sheet items: Add contingent liabilities if material

Why EV Matters:
├── Total cost to acquire a company
├── Capital structure neutral (includes debt)
├── Better than market cap for comparisons
├── Used in EV/EBITDA, EV/FCF, EV/Sales multiples
└── Accounts for cash (cash reduces acquisition cost)
```

## 21.4 Owner Earnings (Buffett's Concept)

```
Owner Earnings = Net Profit + Depreciation & Amortization - Maintenance CapEx

Or equivalently:
Owner Earnings = Operating Cash Flow - Maintenance CapEx

This is the true cash available to owners that can be:
├── Paid as dividends
├── Used for share buybacks
├── Reinvested in business (if ROIC > WACC)
├── Used to pay down debt
└── Held as cash reserves

Example:
Net Profit: ₹500 Cr
D&A: ₹200 Cr
Maintenance CapEx: ₹150 Cr
Growth CapEx: ₹100 Cr
Total CapEx: ₹250 Cr

Owner Earnings = 500 + 200 - 150 = ₹550 Cr
FCF = 500 + 200 - 250 = ₹450 Cr

The ₹100 Cr difference is growth investment creating future value.

Assessment:
├── Owner Earnings growing: Business becoming more valuable
├── Owner Earnings stable: Steady-state business
├── Owner Earnings declining: Business may be maturing
├── Owner Earnings > Reported Profit: Conservative, high quality
└── Owner Earnings < Reported Profit: Maintenance costs high
```

## 21.5 Earnings Yield

```
Earnings Yield = EPS / Price per Share × 100
Earnings Yield = Net Profit / Market Cap × 100

Or for pre-interest comparison:
Earnings Yield = EBIT / Enterprise Value × 100

Interpretation:
├── Compare with bond yields (opportunity cost)
├── Earnings Yield > Bond Yield: Equity may be undervalued
├── Earnings Yield < Bond Yield: Bond may be better value
├── Earnings Yield > 10%: Attractive (for mature companies)
├── Earnings Yield 5-10%: Fair
└── Earnings Yield < 5%: Growth expectations or overvalued

Example:
EPS: ₹25
Price: ₹500
Earnings Yield = 25/500 × 100 = 5%

10-Year Government Bond Yield: 7%
Earnings Yield (5%) < Bond Yield (7%)

Analysis: At current price, bonds offer higher yield than stock earnings.
Stock must deliver >2% capital appreciation to match bond returns.
```

## 21.6 Free Cash Flow Yield

```
FCF Yield = Free Cash Flow / Market Cap × 100
FCF Yield = FCF per Share / Price per Share × 100

Interpretation:
├── FCF Yield > 10%: Potentially undervalued
├── FCF Yield 5-10%: Fairly valued
├── FCF Yield < 5%: Growth expectations or overvalued
├── Compare with bond yields for relative value
└── Compare with historical average for the company

FCF Yield vs Earnings Yield:
├── FCF Yield is more conservative (actual cash vs accounting profit)
├── FCF Yield < Earnings Yield: Company has high CapEx requirements
├── FCF Yield > Earnings Yield: Company has low CapEx (asset-light)
└── FCF Yield is generally more reliable for valuation

Example:
FCF: ₹400 Cr
Market Cap: ₹5,000 Cr
FCF Yield = 400/5,000 × 100 = 8%

10-Year Bond Yield: 7%
FCF Yield (8%) > Bond Yield (7%)
→ Stock offers better cash yield than bonds (positive)
```

## 21.7 Capital Efficiency

```
Capital Efficiency Metrics:

Revenue per Dollar of Capital:
Revenue / Invested Capital = Capital Turnover
Higher = More efficient

Profit per Dollar of Capital:
NOPAT / Invested Capital = ROIC
Higher = More efficient

Cash per Dollar of Capital:
FCF / Invested Capital = FCF/IC
Higher = More efficient

Capital Allocation Score:
├── ROIC vs WACC: Is company creating value?
├── Capital Turnover: Is company using assets efficiently?
├── Growth Rate: Is company growing without destroying returns?
├── FCF Conversion: Is profit converting to cash?
└── Dividend/Buyback: Is excess cash returned to shareholders?

Capital Efficiency by Business Model:
├── Asset-Light (IT, Services): High capital turnover, high ROIC
├── Asset-Heavy (Manufacturing): Lower capital turnover, moderate ROIC
├── Capital-Intensive (Infrastructure): Low capital turnover, regulated returns
└── Platform (Tech): Very high capital turnover, high ROIC (scale)
```

## 21.8 Working Capital Cycle

```
Working Capital Cycle Components:

Receivable Days = Receivables / Revenue × 365
├── Measures how long to collect from customers
├── < 30 days: Excellent
├── 30-60 days: Good
├── 60-90 days: Moderate
├── 90-120 days: Slow
└── > 120 days: Very slow (potential collection issues)

Inventory Days = Inventory / COGS × 365
├── Measures how long inventory sits before sale
├── < 30 days: Excellent (fast-moving goods)
├── 30-60 days: Good
├── 60-90 days: Moderate
├── 90-180 days: Slow (potential obsolescence)
└── > 180 days: Very slow (potential write-offs)

Payable Days = Payables / COGS × 365
├── Measures how long company takes to pay suppliers
├── < 30 days: Very early payment (cash outflow)
├── 30-60 days: Normal
├── 60-90 days: Slow (cash preservation)
├── 90-120 days: Very slow (may strain relationships)
└── > 120 days: May indicate payment difficulties

Net Working Capital Cycle = Receivable Days + Inventory Days - Payable Days
Also called: Cash Conversion Cycle (CCC)

Example:
Receivable Days: 45
Inventory Days: 60
Payable Days: 40
CCC = 45 + 60 - 40 = 65 days

Interpretation:
├── Company needs 65 days of cash to fund operations
├── Lower CCC = Less working capital needed = More efficient
├── Negative CCC = Company gets paid before paying suppliers (ideal)
├── Track CCC trend over time
└── Compare with industry peers
```

## 21.9 Cash Conversion Cycle (CCC)

```
CCC = Receivable Days + Inventory Days - Payable Days

Industry Benchmarks for CCC:
├── IT Services: 30-60 days (low working capital)
├── FMCG: 15-30 days (negative CCC common)
├── Manufacturing: 60-90 days (moderate)
├── Auto: 30-60 days (lean manufacturing)
├── Pharma: 60-120 days (regulatory requirements)
├── Infrastructure: 90-150 days (project-based)
├── Retail: 10-30 days (quick turnover)
└── Real Estate: Variable (pre-sales vs construction)

CCC Analysis Over Time:
├── Declining CCC: Improving efficiency (positive)
├── Rising CCC: Worsening efficiency (negative)
├── Stable CCC: Consistent operations
└── Negative CCC: Company funded by suppliers (very positive)

Impact on Returns:
├── Lower CCC → Lower working capital → Higher ROIC
├── Higher CCC → Higher working capital → Lower ROIC
├── CCC improvement directly improves cash flow
└── CCC deterioration directly hurts cash flow
```

## 21.10 DuPont Analysis (5-Step Breakdown)

```
5-Step DuPont Analysis:

ROE = Tax Burden × Interest Burden × EBIT Margin × Asset Turnover × Equity Multiplier

ROE = (PAT/EBT) × (EBT/EBIT) × (EBIT/Revenue) × (Revenue/Assets) × (Assets/Equity)

Component Analysis:

1. Tax Burden = PAT / EBT
   ├── Measures tax efficiency
   ├── Higher = More profit retained after tax
   ├── Compare with statutory tax rate
   └── May indicate tax planning or one-time tax benefits

2. Interest Burden = EBT / EBIT
   ├── Measures impact of interest expense
   ├── Higher = Less interest burden (less debt)
   ├── Compare with interest coverage ratio
   └── May indicate leverage changes

3. EBIT Margin = EBIT / Revenue
   ├── Measures operational profitability
   ├── Higher = More efficient operations
   ├── Most comparable across companies
   └── Excludes financing and tax effects

4. Asset Turnover = Revenue / Assets
   ├── Measures asset utilization efficiency
   ├── Higher = More efficient use of assets
   ├── Varies significantly by industry
   └── IT >> Banking in asset turnover

5. Equity Multiplier = Assets / Equity
   ├── Measures financial leverage
   ├── Higher = More debt relative to equity
   ├── = 1 + D/E ratio
   └── Increases ROE but also risk

Example:
├── Tax Burden: 0.85 (85% of pre-tax profit retained)
├── Interest Burden: 0.90 (10% lost to interest)
├── EBIT Margin: 0.15 (15% operating margin)
├── Asset Turnover: 0.80 (₹0.80 revenue per ₹1 assets)
├── Equity Multiplier: 2.00 (2x leverage)
│
├── ROE = 0.85 × 0.90 × 0.15 × 0.80 × 2.00
├── ROE = 0.85 × 0.90 × 0.15 × 0.80 × 2.00
├── ROE = 0.1836 = 18.36%
│
└── Analysis:
    ├── Moderate tax efficiency (0.85)
    ├── Low interest burden (0.90) — low leverage
    ├── Decent operational profitability (15%)
    ├── Good asset turnover (0.80)
    └── Moderate leverage (2x)
```

```
│   └── Declining: Lower spending per visit
│
├── Customer Retention Rate: Repeat customers / Total customers
│   ├── Higher = Stronger brand loyalty
│   └── Loyalty program effectiveness
│
└── Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC): Marketing spend / New customers
    ├── Lower = More efficient customer acquisition
    └── Compare with customer lifetime value (LTV)

Inventory Metrics:
├── Inventory Turnover: COGS / Average Inventory
│   ├── Higher = Faster selling, less cash tied up
│   ├── Compare with peers
│   └── Track trend over time
│
├── Inventory Days: 365 / Inventory Turnover
│   ├── Lower = More efficient
│   └── Compare with industry benchmark
│
├── Sell-Through Rate: Units sold / Units received
│   ├── Higher = Better demand forecasting
│   └── Lower = Overstocking or poor demand
│
├── Markdown Rate: Discounted items / Total items
│   ├── Lower = Better brand strength
│   └── Higher = Demand-supply mismatch
│
└── Stock-to-Sales Ratio: Average Inventory / Monthly Sales
    ├── Lower = More lean inventory management
    └── Compare with peers

Margin Metrics:
├── Gross Margin: Revenue - COGS / Revenue
│   ├── Higher = Better pricing or product mix
│   └── Compare with peers
│
├── EBITDA Margin: Operating profitability
├── Store-Level Margin: Contribution margin per store
├── Online vs Offline Margin: Channel profitability comparison
└── Private Label Margin: Typically higher than national brands
```

# 25. COMMON MISTAKES IN FUNDAMENTAL ANALYSIS

## 25.1 Over-Reliance on a Single Metric

```
The Mistake:
├── Judging company solely on PE ratio
├── Making decisions based only on ROE
├── Using only debt-to-equity for assessment
├── Focusing only on revenue growth
└── Evaluating only on dividend yield

Why It Happens:
├── Simplicity bias (one number is easy)
├── Confirmation bias (finding what confirms belief)
├── Anchoring to familiar metric
└── Laziness (not doing comprehensive analysis)

The Correct Approach:
├── Use multiple metrics across categories
│   ├── Quality: ROE, ROCE, ROIC, F-Score, Z-Score
│   ├── Growth: Revenue, Profit, EPS, FCF growth
│   ├── Valuation: PE, PEG, EV/EBITDA, FCF Yield
│   ├── Financial Health: D/E, Interest Coverage, Current Ratio
│   └── Efficiency: Asset Turnover, CCC, Inventory Days
│
├── Create a scoring system (1-5 for each metric)
├── Weight metrics by importance
├── Look for convergence across metrics
└── Never rely on any single number
```

## 25.2 Ignoring Qualitative Factors

```
The Mistake:
├── Focusing only on numbers (ignoring management quality)
├── Ignoring competitive dynamics
├── Not reading annual report narrative
├── Skipping management commentary
└── Ignoring industry trends

Why It Happens:
├── Quantitative data is easier to measure
├── Qualitative analysis requires judgment
├── Emotional comfort with numbers
└── Lack of time or expertise

The Correct Approach:
├── Read every annual report cover to cover
├── Listen to all earnings calls
├── Research management background thoroughly
├── Understand competitive dynamics deeply
├── Track industry trends continuously
├── Visit company operations (if possible)
├── Talk to customers, suppliers, employees
└── Combine quantitative and qualitative for complete picture
```

## 25.3 Confirmation Bias

```
The Mistake:
├── Only seeking information that confirms existing belief
├── Dismissing negative information about owned stocks
├── Ignoring red flags about stocks you want to buy
├── Overweighting positive signals
└── Underweighting negative signals

Why It Happens:
├── Emotional attachment to investment thesis
├── Ego protection (don't want to be wrong)
├── Cognitive dissonance (conflicting information is uncomfortable)
├── Social proof (others agree with you)
└── Sunk cost fallacy (already invested, can't sell)

The Correct Approach:
├── Actively seek disconfirming evidence
├── Make a list of reasons NOT to invest
├── Assign probabilities to both bull and bear cases
├── Set predefined exit criteria before investing
├── Review thesis with fresh eyes periodically
├── Seek out opposing viewpoints
├── Ask: "What would make me change my mind?"
└── If thesis is broken, sell without hesitation
```

## 25.4 Anchoring to Purchase Price

```
The Mistake:
├── "I'll sell when it gets back to my purchase price"
├── Refusing to sell at a loss
├── Adding to losing positions to "average down" without re-analyzing
├── Evaluating current price relative to purchase price (not intrinsic value)
└── Emotional decision-making based on gain/loss

Why It Happens:
├── Loss aversion (losses hurt 2x more than gains feel good)
├── Endowment effect (overvaluing what you own)
├── Anchoring bias (using purchase price as reference point)
├── Regret avoidance (don't want to realize loss)
└── Disposition effect (selling winners too early, holding losers too long)

The Correct Approach:
├── Evaluate current price vs intrinsic value (not purchase price)
├── Ask: "Would I buy this stock today at this price?"
├── If answer is no, consider selling regardless of gain/loss
├── Set stop-loss levels based on thesis, not price
├── Review investment thesis independent of P&L
├── Cut losses quickly when thesis is broken
├── Let winners run (don't sell just because you have a profit)
└── Remember: Sunk costs are irrelevant to future decisions
```

## 25.5 Ignoring Macro Factors

```
The Mistake:
├── Analyzing company in isolation from economy
├── Ignoring interest rate environment
├── Not considering regulatory changes
├── Overlooking currency movements
└── Ignoring geopolitical risks

Why It Happens:
├── Micro focus (company-level analysis is detailed)
├── Macro is complex and uncertain
├── Belief that "good companies do well regardless"
├── Not trained in macro analysis
└── Macro factors seem distant from company fundamentals

The Correct Approach:
├── Always start with macro context (top-down analysis)
├── Understand where we are in economic cycle
├── Track interest rate direction and impact
├── Monitor regulatory environment changes
├── Consider currency exposure for export/import companies
├── Assess geopolitical risks for international operations
├── Understand sector-specific macro drivers
└── Combine top-down (macro) with bottom-up (company) analysis
```

## 25.6 Not Reading the Fine Print

```
The Mistake:
├── Not reading notes to accounts
├── Skipping related party transaction disclosures
├── Ignoring auditor qualifications
├── Not reading risk factors in annual report
├── Skipping MD&A section
├── Not reading proxy statements
└── Ignoring contingent liabilities

Why It Happens:
├── Annual reports are long (200+ pages)
├── Notes to accounts are technical
├── Time pressure
├── Assumption that auditors have caught issues
└── Not knowing what to look for

The Correct Approach:
├── Read every section of annual report systematically
├── Pay special attention to:
│   ├── Notes to accounts (hidden details)
│   ├── Related party transactions (conflicts of interest)
│   ├── Auditor's report (any qualifications?)
│   ├── Risk factors (what company worries about)
│   ├── MD&A (management's own analysis)
│   ├── Contingent liabilities (off-balance sheet risks)
│   ├── Segment reporting (business breakdown)
│   └── Shareholding pattern changes
│
├── Compare with previous year's disclosures
├── Check for changes in accounting policies
├── Look for inconsistencies between narrative and numbers
└── When in doubt, ask the company (investor relations)
```

## 25.7 Overconfidence in DCF Assumptions

```
The Mistake:
├── Using precise numbers in DCF (false precision)
├── Overly optimistic growth assumptions
├── Unrealistic terminal growth rates
├── Underestimating WACC
├── Not performing sensitivity analysis
└── Treating DCF output as exact intrinsic value

Why It Happens:
├── DCF gives a single number (feels precise)
├── Spreadsheet models create illusion of accuracy
├── Optimism bias in projections
├── Anchoring to desired valuation
└── Lack of understanding of DCF limitations

The Correct Approach:
├── Use conservative assumptions
├── Perform sensitivity analysis (vary WACC, growth rate)
├── Create bull, base, and bear scenarios
├── Understand that DCF is an estimate, not exact
├── Use terminal value range (not single point)
├── Compare DCF with other valuation methods
├── Apply margin of safety (25-30%)
├── Remember: DCF is only as good as assumptions
└── Ask: "What assumption am I most uncertain about?"

Example Sensitivity:
├── Bear Case: WACC 14%, Growth 2%, Intrinsic Value: ₹8.1
├── Base Case: WACC 12%, Growth 4%, Intrinsic Value: ₹13.8
├── Bull Case: WACC 10%, Growth 5%, Intrinsic Value: ₹26.3
│
└── Conclusion: Intrinsic value is range ₹8-26, not exact ₹13.8
    Buy at ₹10 or below (margin of safety in base case)
```

## 25.8 Additional Common Mistakes

```
Mistake 9: Chasing Past Performance
├── Buying stocks that have already run up significantly
├── Assuming past returns will continue
├── Ignoring reversion to mean
└── Buying at peaks instead of value

Mistake 10: Not Diversifying
├── Putting too much in single stock
├── Concentrated sector bets
├── No geographic diversification
└── Portfolio risk too high

Mistake 11: Following the Crowd
├── Buying because "everyone is buying"
├── FOMO (Fear of Missing Out)
├── Herd mentality in markets
└── Not doing own analysis

Mistake 12: Timing the Market
├── Trying to buy at exact bottom
├── Trying to sell at exact top

Mistake 13: Ignoring Transaction Costs

Mistake 14: Not Having a System
```

# APPENDIX: FORMULAS QUICK REFERENCE

```
PROFITABILITY:
├── ROE = Net Profit / Equity × 100
├── ROCE = EBIT / Capital Employed × 100
├── ROA = Net Profit / Total Assets × 100
├── ROIC = NOPAT / Invested Capital × 100
├── Gross Margin = Gross Profit / Revenue × 100
├── Operating Margin = EBIT / Revenue × 100
├── EBITDA Margin = EBITDA / Revenue × 100
└── Net Margin = PAT / Revenue × 100

VALUATION:
├── PE = Price / EPS
├── PEG = PE / Growth Rate
├── P/B = Price / Book Value per Share
├── P/S = Market Cap / Revenue
├── EV/EBITDA = (Market Cap + Debt - Cash) / EBITDA
├── EV/Sales = (Market Cap + Debt - Cash) / Revenue
├── FCF Yield = FCF / Market Cap × 100
├── Graham Number = √(22.5 × EPS × BVPS)
└── Earnings Yield = EPS / Price × 100

LIQUIDITY:
├── Current Ratio = Current Assets / Current Liabilities
├── Quick Ratio = (Current Assets - Inventory) / Current Liabilities
└── Cash Ratio = Cash / Current Liabilities

SOLVENCY:
├── D/E = Total Debt / Equity
├── D/A = Total Debt / Total Assets
├── Interest Coverage = EBIT / Interest Expense
└── Debt/EBITDA = Total Debt / EBITDA

EFFICIENCY:
├── Asset Turnover = Revenue / Total Assets
├── Inventory Turnover = COGS / Average Inventory
├── Receivable Turnover = Revenue / Average Receivables
├── Payable Turnover = COGS / Average Payables
├── Inventory Days = 365 / Inventory Turnover
├── Receivable Days = 365 / Receivable Turnover
├── Payable Days = 365 / Payable Turnover
├── CCC = Inventory Days + Receivable Days - Payable Days
└── Working Capital Turnover = Revenue / Working Capital

GROWTH:
├── CAGR = (End Value / Start Value)^(1/Years) - 1
├── YoY Growth = (Current - Previous) / Previous × 100
└── SGR = ROE × Retention Ratio

QUALITY:
├── Piotroski F-Score: 9 criteria, score 0-9
├── Altman Z-Score: 1.2A + 1.4B + 3.3C + 0.6D + 1.0E
├── Beneish M-Score: -4.84 + 0.92DSRI + 0.528GMI + ...
└── Cash Conversion = OCF / Net Profit

CASH FLOW:
├── FCF = OCF - CapEx
├── Owner Earnings = Net Profit + D&A - Maintenance CapEx
├── Cash EBITDA = EBITDA - CapEx
└── FCFF = EBIT(1-T) + D&A - ΔWC - CapEx

DCF:
├── WACC = (E/V × Ke) + (D/V × Kd × (1-T))
├── Ke (CAPM) = Rf + β(Rm - Rf)
├── Terminal Value = FCF × (1+g) / (WACC - g)
├── Enterprise Value = Σ PV(FCFs) + PV(Terminal Value)
├── Equity Value = EV - Debt + Cash
├── Intrinsic Value = Equity Value / Shares
└── Buy Price = Intrinsic Value × (1 - Margin of Safety)
```
